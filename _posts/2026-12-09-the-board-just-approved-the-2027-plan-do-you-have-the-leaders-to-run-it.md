Sometime in the last few weeks, or the next two, a board approved a 2027 plan. It has a revenue target, a margin target, a capital line for the new suite, and a headcount number that HR will turn into requisitions in January. What it almost never has is a page that says whether the eight or ten people expected to deliver it can. Budget season produces a plan. It rarely produces a clear look at the bench that has to run it.

<!--more-->

The leadership market this plan launches into is not calm. Russell Reynolds, which tracks 13 global indices, recorded 234 CEO departures in 2025, up 16 percent from 2024 and 21 percent above the eight-year average. Spencer Stuart counted 168 new CEOs named across the S&P 1500, the most since 2010. One level down, the churn is faster: the same firm's December 2025 snapshot of S&P 500 C-suites put average tenure at 5.2 years, and chief operating officers, the people who most often own the plan day to day, at 3.3 years.

Those are large public companies, but the private manufacturers, defense contractors, and life sciences firms we work with hire from the same pool and lose people to it. A plan built on the assumption that none of your leaders will leave, and that all of them can do what it asks, carries an unexamined risk on page one.

We covered the broader rebuild in our January 2026 piece on the leadership reset, and the mechanics of succession in February 2026. This article is narrower: one check, done once, between plan approval and the January board meeting.

## Why plan approval is the moment

Before the plan is approved, "can she run it" is an abstract question. After, it is specific. The plan says the Durham site adds a second shift by June. It says the company files its first 510(k), the FDA's premarket clearance for a medical device, in the third quarter. Each line has a name attached, and each name has a track record that either includes that kind of work or does not. December is the first month the question can be asked with precision.

Most boards have not asked it. NACD's 2024 survey of private company directors found 69 percent had discussed long-term CEO succession in the past year, yet 36 percent said their board would be unprepared if the CEO left tomorrow, and another 10 percent were unsure. Public company boards did better on the top seat, with 71 percent naming an interim for an emergency, but 30 percent still had no successor identified.

Heidrick & Struggles asked 1,921 CEOs and board members for its 2026 CEO and Board Confidence Monitor, published in February. Only four in ten expressed confidence that their CEO succession planning positioned the organization well for the future, and North American respondents tended to express lower confidence than other regions. That is the single most planned-for job in the company. The roles below it usually have no plan at all.

DDI's 2025 Global Leadership Forecast, built on responses from 2,185 HR professionals and nearly 11,000 leaders, found 20 percent of HR professionals confident in the strength of their leadership bench, up from 12 percent in the 2023 edition. Four in five still are not.

There is also a calendar reason. Challenger, Gray & Christmas counted 209 CEO departures at U.S. companies in January 2026, up 40 percent from December. Our November 18 article walked through why that wave forms in the fall. A gap check in December catches the leader who is already halfway out the door. It also catches the leader who is staying but cannot do what the plan now requires, the quieter and more common problem.

## The four questions

The check we run with clients fits on one page. Every leader whose departure or underperformance would put a plan line at risk gets a row, and each row gets four questions. It is a practical frame rather than a research instrument, and it works because it forces specific answers.

**Can they run this plan?** Not "are they good." Most are, or they would not be there. The question is whether 2027 asks for something this person has never done. Running a plant is different from doubling it. Managing a quality system is different from taking it through its first FDA inspection. Winning task orders is different from leading a prime bid, where your company holds the government contract directly instead of working under another firm. Write down the one new thing the plan requires of this leader, then the evidence they have done something like it. An empty second box is a gap.

**Do they want to?** Some leaders can run the plan and have already decided not to. The bonus is the usual timer: a late-2025 poll of 2,000 U.S. workers by JobHire AI, a job-search app, found 48 percent of those expecting an annual bonus were already considering their exit (a signal, not a study). The only way to answer this is to ask the person, in a scheduled conversation, before year-end. For this check, the entry is one word in a box: yes, unsure, or no.

**Who backs them up?** A name, not a process. The NACD data says 71 percent of public company boards have an interim CEO identified. In our experience, almost none have one named for the head of manufacturing, the head of regulatory affairs, or the facility security officer, the person who keeps a defense contractor's clearance paperwork in order. The backup does not have to be ready to take the job permanently. They have to hold it for the four months a search takes without the plan line slipping. If nobody fits that sentence, the box is red.

**What breaks if they leave?** One sentence per leader, as concrete as possible. The FDA inspection in the second quarter has no one who has hosted one. The recompete, the rebid of a contract the company already holds, loses the only person the contracting officer knows. The new line's commissioning depends on a vendor relationship that lives in one person's phone. This question turns a succession exercise into a risk register.

Eight to twelve names, four columns, one page. The two or three red boxes are the agenda for January.

## A gap you develop and a gap you hire

Once a gap is visible, the choice is develop, hire, or bridge. The data favors development more than most CEOs expect, with one exception.

Boards choose insiders most of the time. Spencer Stuart's 2025 S&P 1500 data shows 60 percent of new CEOs were internal, and 73 percent in the S&P 500. The split narrows as companies get smaller, with SmallCap 600 boards at 50-50, closer to what we see in the mid-market. Across the S&P 500 C-suite as a whole, 59 percent were promoted from inside.

There is a reason, though the evidence is old. A 2008 Institute of Executive Development study, cited by McKinsey in a 2018 review of leadership transitions, reported 92 percent of external hires and 72 percent of internal ones took far more than 90 days to reach full productivity, and 62 percent of external hires said it took at least six months to have real impact, against 25 percent of internal promotions. Those figures are nearly two decades old.

They match a Wharton study reported in 2012, built on personnel records from 2003 to 2009 at an investment bank: outside recruits earned 18 to 20 percent more than insiders promoted into similar jobs, received lower performance evaluations for their first two years, and left at higher rates. Both studies are dated and neither was run at a manufacturer, so treat them as context, not a rule.

The exception is capability the company has never had. An internal candidate can grow into more scope. They cannot grow, in six months, into a sterile fill-finish startup, a first commercial launch, or a first prime contract if nobody in the building has done one. That is a hire. So is a gap that must close by March when the candidate needs a year of coaching.

The third path, a bridge, is an interim or contract executive who holds the seat while an insider develops or a search runs. It keeps the plan line from slipping.

Whichever path you pick, the failure risk is real. The same McKinsey review, citing several studies, put the share of executive transitions regarded as failures or disappointments after two years at between 27 and 46 percent. Newer data points the same way: Russell Reynolds found the share of CEOs departing within 30 to 36 months of appointment rose 79 percent in 2025. A December gap check does not remove that risk. It gives the company a year of runway to manage it.

## The lead-time math

Clockwork, a software vendor for search firms, analyzed tens of thousands of retained search projects run from 2020 through 2023 and put the global average at 117 days from kickoff to placement, just under four months. Board searches ran 155 days. That is vendor data, not independent research. In our own practice, board approval adds two to six weeks at the end of a search, and executive notice periods run from two weeks to three months.

Now run the calendar. A search launched the second week of January, with a written spec and a booked interview panel, reaches placement in early May on the 117-day average. Add a notice period, and the new leader starts between late May and August. Add the six months most outside hires in the 2008 study said they needed to have real impact, and the plan gets its new leader's full effect in the fourth quarter at best. The sitting executives you want are also waiting for their own bonus, so a February yes often means an April arrival.

Compare that with the replacement search. A leader resigns in January. The company spends February on the counteroffer and March on the interim. The search opens in April or May. On the same arithmetic, placement lands in August or September, and the start date falls anywhere from late August to December. By then the 2027 plan has been run by an interim for most of the year, and the person who finally arrives is building 2028. Open it in June and arrival slips to year-end.

The difference between those two timelines is whether the gap was found in December or in June.

Life sciences companies should read this section twice. Spencer Stuart's 2025 data put CEO turnover at 14 percent in healthcare, tied for the highest of any sector, against 11 percent in industrials, and healthcare C-suites were tied for the least likely to promote from inside, at 56 percent versus 61 percent for industrials. Our inference is that life sciences boards go outside more often, so the lead-time math bites them hardest.

## What sophisticated boards and CEOs do differently

**They add a leadership page to the plan.** One page, approved with the budget, listing the leaders the plan depends on and the four-question answers for each, reviewed at the same meetings as the financials.

**They separate "good leader" from "right leader for this plan."** The site head who ran a stable plant for six years may not be the person to double it. Saying so in December is a development conversation. Saying so in September is a replacement.

**They name an interim for every critical seat.** The NACD data shows boards do this for the top job. In our experience, they stop there. The roles that break a plan are usually two levels down.

**They choose develop, hire, or bridge before the gap is public, and start the clock in December.** The decision is cheaper and the options wider when nobody has resigned yet. A search gets its spec, its panel, and its briefed search partner before the holidays, so a January launch is a launch.

## What this means for boards and CEOs

First, bring the one-page grid to the January board meeting. Every leader the plan depends on, four columns, color-coded. If the grid does not exist, the finding is that the plan was approved without one.

Second, for every red box, name the path and the date. Develop, with a stretch assignment and a coach, starting in the first quarter. Hire, with a search launched in the first two weeks of January. Bridge, with an interim identified now, in case the first two paths run late.

Third, put a mid-year recheck on the calendar. Our July 8, 2026 piece covered the mid-year reset for the hiring plan. The leadership page needs the same review, because the plan will change by June and so will some of the answers.

The board approved a number. That number assumes a set of people will do a set of things, most of which they have done before and some they have not. Finding out which is which takes one page and three weeks in December. Finding out in June costs a search, a notice period, a ramp, and most of the year the plan was written for.
