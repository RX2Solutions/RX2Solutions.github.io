Every year in the first week of December, hiring managers across manufacturing, life sciences, and defense make the same call. The open requisition can wait. Half the interview panel is out, the budget is closing, and nobody wants to run a search over the holidays. They will pick it back up in January. Then January arrives, and they find themselves recruiting in the most crowded month of the year.

<!--more-->

The numbers behind that crowd are consistent. Indeed's Hiring Lab found that job searches on its platform ran up to 31 percent higher in January 2026 than the early-December 2025 average, a spike that has shown up every January since 2020 except the pandemic-disrupted January of 2021. Job postings did not follow. The same analysis found postings never rose above their early-December level at any point in January 2026. More people looking, the same number of jobs. The most recent Robert Half survey, released in June 2026, found 46 percent of employed U.S. workers planned to look for a new position in the second half of the year, up from 38 percent six months earlier.

Here is the part most hiring plans miss. A candidate who accepts an offer in the second week of December starts in January. A requisition that opens on January 5 lands a start date in March. The gap is a quarter of capacity, decided in early December by a manager who believes the market has gone quiet.

## Where the belief comes from

The belief is half right, which is why it survives. The Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes its Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey two ways: seasonally adjusted, which strips out the calendar, and not seasonally adjusted, which shows it. The adjusted headline numbers showed hires little changed from December 2025 (5.27 million) to January 2026 (5.35 million after revision). The raw figures tell a different story. Employers completed 3.9 million hires in December 2025 and 5.3 million in January 2026, a jump of more than a third.

December had the fewest hires of any month in each year from 2022 through 2025. In 2025 the typical month saw about 5.2 million, so December ran roughly a quarter below normal. Manufacturing shows a sharper version of the pattern. Unadjusted hires in the sector fell to 182,000 in December 2025, then rebounded to 313,000 in January, about 72 percent higher. JOLTS counts positions open on the last business day of the month, and the raw count for December 2025 was 6.1 million, the lowest reading of the year. By the end of January it was 7.4 million.

So yes, less hiring gets done in December. But look at what those figures measure. They count hires employers completed and openings employers posted. They say nothing about whether candidates were available. A requisition closed with the budget, an interview panel scattered across two weeks of vacation, an offer held until the new plan is signed: those are employer decisions. The December trough is a record of companies pausing, not of talent disappearing. Daniel Zhao, now Glassdoor's chief economist, wrote in 2019 that November and December slow down because workers take time off from their job searches, and that January then brought 22 percent more job applications than a typical month in the United States, against 4 percent fewer new openings.

## What candidates do while employers wait

Candidates do not leave the market in December. They wait for it to reopen, and when it does, they all arrive at once. Indeed's data shows search activity started stronger in January 2026 than a year earlier, then faded in the second half of the month. Its Job Postings Index fell through 2025, from 11.5 percent above its pre-pandemic baseline on January 1 to 4.7 percent above on December 31, then sat flat through the first three weeks of January 2026. Some of the steepest January posting declines came in retail, driving, and logistics support, where seasonal holiday jobs were winding down.

The crowd knows it is a crowd. In Robert Half's December 2025 survey, 68 percent of job seekers between roles expected their search to take longer than previous ones, and 59 percent cited too many applicants and competition. Employers feel the same volume from the other side. January brings more applications per opening, not better ones, and the screening load lands on the recruiters who were told to wait.

One caution. We have not seen survey data showing candidates respond faster in December. What we observe in recruiting practice is that the people who return a call in the second week of December have already decided to move. The January pool includes everyone whose resolution was to look. Treat that as a working observation from the field, not a statistic.

## The calendar math of a January start

SHRM's 2026 Recruiting Executives Benchmarking, drawn from more than 4,600 organizations, put median time-to-fill for nonexecutive roles at 39 calendar days, down from the prior year. Executive time-to-fill was unchanged from the prior year, when it was 45 days. The clock runs in calendar days from the day the requisition opens to the day the candidate accepts, so weekends and holidays are already in that number. The notice period is not.

Run the dates. A nonexecutive requisition opened January 5 reaches an accepted offer around February 13 at the median. Add a notice period and the person walks in the door in March. That assumes the process holds its pace through the January volume. Open the same search on December 1 and the median lands an accepted offer around January 9, with a late-January or February start. Better still, a search already moving in November, with finalists interviewing in early December, produces an offer before the holidays. In our experience, candidates who accept in mid-December usually ask to start after the new year anyway. Both sides want the same date.

The cost of the slower path is not abstract. Robert Half's survey of more than 2,000 hiring managers, fielded in April 2026 and released in July, found 63 percent had experienced significant project delays because they lacked staff with the right skills, and 48 percent had canceled projects outright. Two thirds planned to increase permanent hiring in the second half of 2026, up from 57 percent a year before. If those plans hold, the roles still open in December will restart in January, most of them in the same few weeks.

## The contractor angle: December starts protect Q1

Contract staffing did not stop in December 2025. The American Staffing Association's weekly index for December 8 through 14 showed new assignment starts up 3.0 percent from the prior week, and 42 percent of firms surveyed reported week-to-week gains in new assignments, equal to the 2025 average. The SIA Bullhorn Staffing Indicator showed professional staffing hours holding level through the weeks ending December 13 and December 20, after hitting year-to-date peaks in the run-up to Thanksgiving, then dropping 23.7 percent in Christmas week. The dead zone is the week of Christmas, not the month of December.

That matters because contract capacity is what most hiring managers reach for when a project is late. In the same Robert Half survey, 56 percent expected to bring in contract professionals in the second half of 2026. The reasons given for using a staffing firm were specialized project skills (60 percent), urgent project support (56 percent), and backfilling internal staff (48 percent). Every one of those reasons is more urgent on January 4 than it was on December 4.

A contractor who starts the second week of December is trained, badged, and productive when the 2027 plan goes live. One requested on January 5 starts in late January and is productive in February. For a regulated plant, that is the difference between having a validation engineer or quality reviewer on the floor for year-end batch record review and first-quarter audit prep, or not.

For defense contractors, December is not the customer's budget year-end at all. The federal fiscal year runs October 1 through September 30, so a December contract start lands in the government's first quarter. And as our September 30, 2026 piece on contract-to-hire laid out, that start doubles as a working interview that can convert to a permanent hire in the spring.

## How to run a tight December process

The December process works only if it is compressed on purpose. The first move happens before the month starts: pull the vacation calendar for every interviewer and build the interview schedule around it. In our experience, December searches die because nobody checked who would be out the week of the 14th. Assign a backup for every panel seat, and collapse rounds where you can. Our July 22, 2026 article on interview process length made the case for fewer, better-designed interviews, and December is where that pays off.

Settle the offer authority in advance. The compensation band, the approver, and the start-date flexibility should all be decided before the first interview, so an offer can go out the same week as the final conversation. Then keep the loop warm through the holiday week. In SHRM's 2025 Talent Trends survey, 41 percent of organizations said they were seeing candidates ghost them during the interview process. Candidates ghost processes that go silent first. A short note before December 24 with the plan for early January keeps a finalist from reading a quiet week as a rejection.

Where an offer cannot happen before year-end, December still has a job. Screens and first interviews run through the third week of the month, so the first week of January is the final round, not the kickoff.

## What to say to a hiring manager who wants to wait

The manager says nobody is around and candidates will be more responsive in January. This is the response that works.

First, the timing. If we open this on January 5, the median time-to-fill puts an accepted offer in mid-February and a start in March. If we run it now, the offer goes out before the holidays and the person starts in January. You are choosing between January and March, not between December and January.

Second, the crowd. Searches on Indeed ran up to 31 percent higher in January than early December last winter, and postings stayed flat. Your posting will compete with every other restart on the same day. In December it competes with fewer.

Third, the panel. We know who is out and when, and the interview schedule is built around it with backups named. The process does not need everyone in the building. It needs the right four people for an hour each.

Fourth, the fallback. If it is clear by December 4 that a permanent offer will not close before the holidays, a contractor starts the week of December 7 so the project has coverage when the plan goes live.

## What sophisticated employers do differently

**They read the unadjusted numbers.** Leaders who know December hires run a quarter below a normal month, and that the gap is employer behavior, treat the month as a window instead of a pause.

**They set a December offer deadline instead of a January restart date.** The planning question is which open roles can reach an accepted offer by the third week of December, and what has to happen this week to make that true.

**They pre-book the panel in November.** Interview slots for the first two weeks of December go on calendars before Thanksgiving, with named backups. A search that depends on finding an hour across six calendars in mid-December will not find it.

**They use contract starts to protect the quarter.** When a permanent hire cannot close before the holidays, a contractor starts in early December so the role is covered when the plan launches. The first-quarter deliverable does not wait for the March start.

## What this means for boards and CEOs

First, ask for the list of open and approved roles as of December 1, and for each one, the date the last candidate was interviewed. A role with no interview activity in December is a role that will be filled in March.

Second, require every first-quarter project in the 2027 plan to name its staffing source by mid-December: a permanent hire with an accepted offer, a contractor with a confirmed start date, or an internal reassignment. A project whose answer is "we will post it in January" needs a different answer.

Third, treat the December interview calendar as a leadership commitment. If the CEO's direct reports are not available for a finalist interview in the second week of December, neither is anyone below them.

December is the lowest-hire month of the year because employers make it one. The candidates are still there, the jobs they will chase in January are the same ones sitting unposted in December, and the time-to-fill clock does not care which month it starts in. A company that keeps its process running through the third week of December makes offers that become January starts. A company that waits makes January postings that become March starts. The market is not dead in December. It is just unattended.
