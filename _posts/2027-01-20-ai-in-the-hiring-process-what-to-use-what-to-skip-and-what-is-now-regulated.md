SHRM's 2026 State of AI in HR research, drawn from 1,908 HR professionals, found 39 percent of organizations using AI in their HR functions, with recruiting the most common use at 27 percent. The same study found that in the 19 states with AI-related workplace laws on the books, 57 percent of HR professionals were unaware of them. Most adoption did not start in a strategy meeting. It arrived inside the applicant tracking system, as a feature the vendor switched on or a chatbot that began answering candidates before anyone decided it should. For a manufacturer or a life sciences company with a three-person HR team, the question for 2027 is whether you know where AI is already running and can explain what it does.

<!--more-->

## What is in use

SHRM's 2024 survey of 2,366 HR professionals found that among organizations using AI for HR, 64 percent applied it to recruiting, interviewing, and hiring, most often to generate job descriptions (65 percent) and far less often to screen resumes (33 percent). Those figures are three years old; use them for direction, not scale.

The candidate side looks different. Greenhouse's 2026 Candidate AI Interview Report, a vendor survey of 1,200 U.S. job seekers within a five-country study published in May 2026, found 63 percent had been interviewed by an AI, up 13 points in six months. iHire's 2026 State of Online Recruiting survey of more than 300 U.S. employers and 1,000 job seekers put employer use of AI in hiring at 24 percent.

## Where it helps

The uses that save time without making a decision are easy: drafting a job description, scheduling interviews, answering a candidate's benefits question at 9 p.m., building a sourcing list.

Speed is not guaranteed. ManpowerGroup's fourth-quarter 2026 Employment Outlook Survey of nearly 40,000 employers in 42 countries found 28 percent said time-to-hire was faster than in 2025, 41 percent about the same, and 30 percent slower. The tool accelerates whatever process you already have, including the broken parts.

## Where it hurts

**Bias is measurable.** A University of Washington study presented in October 2024 ran three large language models through more than three million comparisons of real resumes and job listings, changing only the applicant's name. The models favored white-associated names 85 percent of the time and Black-associated names 9 percent. They preferred male-associated names 52 percent of the time and female-associated names 11 percent.

**Candidates walk.** Among U.S. job seekers, Greenhouse found 38 percent had abandoned a hiring process because it included an AI interview, 70 percent were not clearly told upfront that AI would evaluate them, and 51 percent of those who completed one had not received an outcome. iHire found 44 percent of job seekers view employers that use AI in hiring negatively, against about 22 percent who see it as a plus. For a company without a household name, which our September 9, 2026 article covered, that is a real cost.

**Nobody can explain the decision.** In SHRM's 2024 survey, only 34 percent of organizations buying AI tools said their vendor was very transparent about how it prevents bias. Under the Colorado and California rules below, that is a compliance gap.

**Candidates are using AI too.** iHire found about 24 percent of employers received at least one fake applicant in the past year. AI screening is now reading AI writing, which makes the structured interviews and work samples in our October 21, 2026 piece on skills-based hiring more valuable than before.

## The compliance map as of January 2027

Every date below was checked against a primary source or a law firm alert before publication. Seven U.S. jurisdictions, the federal government, and the EU now have rules that reach AI in hiring, and they ask for different things.

**New York City.** Local Law 144 has been enforced since July 5, 2023. An employer using an automated employment decision tool for hiring or promotion needs a bias audit within the past year covering sex, race and ethnicity, and their intersections, a public summary of it, and notice to candidates ten business days before use. A December 2025 state Comptroller review called the city's enforcement ineffective, and no public enforcement actions had been announced as of October 2026.

**New Jersey.** The Division on Civil Rights adopted disparate impact regulations, N.J.A.C. 13:16, effective December 15, 2025. They codify the state's burden-shifting framework and name automated decision-making tools as a potential source of disparate impact liability, with an AI system trained on a company's existing workforce as the example. For a Pennsylvania company, this is the closest rule in force.

**Illinois.** Two laws apply. The Artificial Intelligence Video Interview Act, in force since January 2020, requires notice, an explanation of how the AI works, and consent before AI analyzes a recorded video interview, plus deletion within 30 days of a request. HB 3773 amended the Illinois Human Rights Act effective January 1, 2026. It makes it a civil rights violation to use AI in a way that has a discriminatory effect on a protected class, bars zip codes as a proxy for one, and requires notice when AI is used in employment decisions. The state proposed notice rules in May 2026, then postponed them on June 2; the statute is in force without them.

**California.** The Civil Rights Council's regulations on automated-decision systems took effect October 1, 2025. They make an employer's anti-bias testing relevant evidence in a discrimination claim, require four years of ADS records, and treat an agent such as a recruiting vendor as an employer under state law. The California Privacy Protection Agency's automated decisionmaking rules reached their compliance date on January 1, 2027. For businesses covered by the state privacy law, they require notice before an automated tool helps make a significant decision about hiring, pay, promotion or termination, a risk assessment, a right to opt out or appeal to a human reviewer, and a right to an explanation. SB 947 adds a bar on discipline or termination based solely on an automated system, operative July 1, 2027.

**Connecticut.** SB 5, signed May 27, 2026, works in two steps. Since October 1, 2026, using an automated employment decision tool is no defense to a discrimination complaint, anti-bias testing may be considered as evidence, and layoff notices must disclose whether the cuts relate to AI. From October 1, 2027, employers must tell applicants and employees when they are interacting with AI and give written notice, naming the tool and the data it analyzes, when AI is a substantial factor in an employment decision. The attorney general enforces it; there is no private right of action.

**Colorado.** SB 26-189, signed May 14, 2026, replaced Colorado's 2024 AI Act. It applies to consequential decisions, including hiring and pay, made on or after January 1, 2027, and swaps the original impact assessments for notice before a covered tool is used, a plain-language disclosure within 30 days of an adverse decision explaining the tool's role, a right to correct inaccurate data and request human review, and three years of records. The attorney general enforces it, with no private right of action and a 60-day cure period for most violations.

The statutory date and the enforcement date differ. On April 9, 2026, xAI sued in federal court in Colorado, and on April 24 the U.S. Department of Justice intervened on xAI's side. On April 27 the court approved a stipulation under which the attorney general will not enforce the law while the challenge is pending, with a 14-day buffer after any ruling on xAI's preliminary injunction motion, due within 28 days of final rulemaking. Proposed rules were published August 11, 2026, with a hearing October 26. Check the status of the rules and the case before relying on either date.

**Texas.** The Responsible Artificial Intelligence Governance Act, HB 149, took effect January 1, 2026. For private employers it is narrow: it bans developing or deploying AI with intent to discriminate against a protected class, and it states that disparate impact alone does not prove a violation. Only the attorney general can enforce it, with penalties from $10,000 to $200,000 per violation and a 60-day cure period.

**Federal.** The federal government has moved in the other direction from the states. In January 2025 the EEOC removed its AI guidance from its website, and the OFCCP guidance for federal contractors went with it. An April 23, 2025 order directed federal agencies to step back from disparate impact enforcement. On December 11, 2025, Executive Order 14365 created an AI Litigation Task Force to challenge state AI laws that conflict with federal policy, directed the Commerce Department to list state laws it considers onerous, and tied certain federal funds to state alignment. On June 9, 2026, the Justice Department's Office of Legal Counsel found the EEOC's disparate impact guidelines unconstitutional. That opinion binds federal agencies, not courts or private plaintiffs. Title VII's text still includes disparate impact, and state laws still apply. As Seyfarth attorney Rachel See put it, "taking it down doesn't change discrimination laws or employer obligations."

**The courts.** Mobley v. Workday, filed in 2023 in federal court in California, is the case to watch. On July 12, 2024 the court let the disparate impact claims proceed, reasoning that a screening vendor can be liable as the employer's agent. On May 16, 2025 Judge Rita Lin preliminarily certified a collective action under the federal age discrimination law, and on June 22, 2026 she granted in part and denied in part Workday's latest motion to dismiss. In September 2026 the plaintiffs moved to certify a class with subclasses by race, gender, age, and disability. The vendor's name on the software does not move the liability off your desk.

**Europe.** For companies with EU operations, the AI Act classifies recruitment and worker management tools, including CV-sorting software, as high-risk. The Digital Omnibus on AI, published July 24, 2026, moved the compliance date for those systems from August 2, 2026 to December 2, 2027. Deployers will need human oversight.

## The vendor questions and the paper trail

Most mid-size employers will inherit all of this from the ATS. Five questions sort out a vendor: which features score, rank, or filter candidates, and whether each can be switched off; whether the model has been tested for adverse impact by sex, race, and age, with results you can see; whether it can explain, for any rejected applicant, what it did; whether the contract supports the state audit, disclosure, and retention rules; and who carries liability if it fails, keeping in mind that Colorado voids indemnification clauses used to shield either side from discrimination liability.

The paper trail matters more than the policy: an inventory of every tool that touches a hiring decision, a written notice to every applicant, a record of each adverse impact test and the response, a named human who reviews before a rejection goes out, and files kept four years to satisfy the strictest state. With the OFCCP guidance gone, that file is a defense contractor's only evidence of good faith.

## What sophisticated employers do differently

**They let AI do the paperwork and keep the decision human.** Job descriptions, scheduling, candidate messages, sourcing lists: automate freely. Any step that moves a person forward or out gets a named reviewer with authority to overrule the tool. That one rule covers the human review piece of Colorado's law, California's appeal right, and the EU's oversight requirement, though each asks for more.

**They test before a regulator or a plaintiff does.** An adverse impact check by sex, race, and age, run at least yearly on any tool that scores candidates, with the result and response filed. California and Connecticut treat that record as evidence in your favor. New York City requires an annual audit.

**They tell candidates.** A plain notice in the posting and the application that says which steps use AI and how to request a human review. Greenhouse found 57 percent of job seekers think disclosure should be required by law.

## What this means for boards and CEOs

First, ask for the inventory at the next operating review: every hiring tool that scores, ranks, or filters applicants, the states it is used in, and the name of the person who reviews its output. If no one can produce the list in a week, that is the finding.

Second, assign the compliance file to one owner with a budget: notices, annual adverse impact tests, vendor attestations, and four-year retention.

Third, decide the policy on AI-scored interviews and rejections before a vendor decides it for you. More than a third of candidates have walked away from a hiring process over an AI interview, and half never got an answer.

The risk is not the technology. It is a hiring decision no one in the building can explain, made by a tool no one in the building chose. The recurring asks across these laws are notice, testing, records, and a human who can overrule the tool. No single law requires all four, Texas requires none, and the federal government is contesting several of them in court. The companies that build the file anyway will be ready for whichever version survives.
