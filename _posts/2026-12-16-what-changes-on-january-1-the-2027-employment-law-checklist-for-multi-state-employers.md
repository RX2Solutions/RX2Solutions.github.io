Picture a company with 40 people in New Jersey, 30 in Maryland, 25 in Massachusetts, and a plant in Pennsylvania. On January 1, 2027, four different things happen to its payroll. New Jersey's minimum wage rises 56 cents to $16.48. Maryland starts taking paid leave deductions for the first time. Massachusetts flips who pays for which piece of its paid leave program. Pennsylvania changes nothing. The system has to know every one of them by the first pay date of the year, and a lot of companies learn which ones it missed in February, when an employee question lands in HR's inbox.

<!--more-->

A note on method before the list. Every item below was checked against a state or federal government page or an alert from a major employment law firm as of mid-October 2026. Where a number had not been published yet, we say so instead of guessing. Laws get delayed and agencies post late, so confirm each line with your own labor department before the first payroll of the year runs.

January 1 draws so many changes because so many clocks reset with the calendar: indexed minimum wages, payroll wage bases, plan-year limits, and the effective dates legislatures like to pick. One date carries more compliance weight than the rest of the year combined.

## State by state: what we could confirm

**Pennsylvania.** Minimum wage stays at the federal $7.25. H.B. 2189, which would raise it to $11 in 2027, passed the House in March 2026 and had not moved in the Senate as of mid-October. We found no state-level employment law change scheduled for January 1, 2027. For a company headquartered here, the work is in the other states.

**New Jersey.** The state Department of Labor announced in September that the minimum wage rises $0.56 to $16.48 on January 1, 2027. Seasonal and small employers (fewer than six employees) move to $16.25, agricultural workers to $15.00, and direct care staff at long-term care facilities to $19.48. The tipped cash wage climbs to $6.61, with the tip credit held at $9.87. The taxable wage base for temporary disability and family leave insurance increases to $177,100 for 2027 from $171,100, and the department's page said the 2027 worker contribution rates would be posted later this year.

**Delaware.** Minimum wage holds at $15.00. Paid leave is already live: contributions began January 1, 2025, and benefits started January 1, 2026, with coverage tiers by employer size and payments capped at $900 per week. The state's pay transparency law, HB 105, was signed September 26, 2025 and takes effect two years after enactment, which puts it in late September 2027. It will cover employers with 26 or more employees and require a pay range plus a general description of benefits in postings.

**Maryland.** Minimum wage holds at $15.00. The big change is FAMLI, the state's paid family and medical leave insurance program. After repeated delays, payroll deductions and employer contributions begin January 1, 2027, at a total rate of 0.90 percent of wages up to the Social Security wage base, which SSA set at $192,300 for 2027. Employers may withhold up to half from employees. Those with fewer than 15 employees skip the employer share but still remit the employee half.

Contributions get sent in quarterly starting April 2027. Benefits of up to $1,000 per week are scheduled to become available no later than January 3, 2028. Companies that wanted a private plan had a window to file a declaration of intent between September 1 and November 15, 2026. If no filing was made, assume the state program applies.

**New York.** The statewide minimum wage does not change on January 1, 2027. It stays at $17.00 in New York City, Long Island, and Westchester, and $16.00 in the rest of the state. The governor's office has proposed raising those to $17.60 and $16.55 on March 1, 2027, if the Legislature approves, so this one may move in the first quarter instead. Home care aide minimums do rise on January 1, to $20.00 and $19.00. Paid Family Leave also shifts: the 2027 employee contribution is 0.452 percent of wages, with an annual cap of $451.81, up from $411.91.

**Massachusetts.** Minimum wage holds at $15.00. The paid leave contribution rate stays at 0.88 percent for 2027, but who pays what flips on January 1. Under a 2026 law, the family leave piece becomes 0.70 of wages, and employers with 25 or more covered workers must pay 60 percent of it. The medical portion drops to 0.18 and can be withheld fully from employees. The employer share remains 0.42 in total. What changes is the line it sits on, which matters for payroll setup and for any private plan written around the old split.

**North Carolina.** Minimum wage stays at $7.25. Nothing on our list changes in North Carolina on January 1.

**California.** Minimum wage rises to $17.40 on January 1, 2027, from $16.90. Because the state's salary test for exempt employees is set at twice that rate, the floor becomes $72,384. California also has the largest batch of new employment laws taking effect that day, and three deserve a line on any multi-state list.

The first is the California Privacy Protection Agency's automated decisionmaking rules, which reach their compliance date on January 1, 2027. For businesses covered by the state privacy law (in practice, mid-sized and larger for-profit employers), they require a pre-use notice before an automated tool helps make a significant decision about hiring, work assignment, pay, promotion or termination, a documented risk assessment, a right to opt out or appeal to a human reviewer, and a right to an explanation of how the tool reached its decision. These sit alongside the state's civil rights regulations on automated decision systems, in effect since October 1, 2025, which require four-year retention of the related records.

The second is SB 951, which amends Cal/WARN so that a layoff notice must say when AI or other automation substantially caused the layoff. The third is SB 1149, which extends bereavement leave to a designated person. Also note SB 947, the No Robo Bosses Act, which bars discipline or termination based solely on an automated system, with its main duties operative July 1, 2027.

## The federal numbers

Several federal figures reset every January. One of the big ones, the IRS retirement limits, had not been published when this was written.

Social Security wage base. SSA set the 2027 base at $192,300 on October 14, up from $184,500. It also caps Maryland's FAMLI contributions.

Retirement plan limits. For 2026, the 401(k) deferral limit is $24,500, the catch-up for people 50 and over is $8,000, the higher amount for ages 60 to 63 is $11,250, and the IRA cap is $7,500. The IRS released those on November 13, 2025. Expect the 2027 figures on the same schedule.

ACA affordability. Revenue Procedure 2026-26 set the required contribution percentage for plan years beginning in 2027 at 10.22 percent, up from 9.96 percent in 2026. Employers that lean on a safe harbor should rerun the math on the employee share of the lowest-cost plan.

Overtime salary threshold. No change. The Department of Labor rescinded the 2024 rule effective May 15, 2026, after a federal court in Texas vacated it. The federal floor for the executive, administrative, and professional exemptions stays at $684 per week, and the highly compensated employee level holds at $107,432. State floors run higher, with California at $72,384, so a multi-state employer sets exempt pay by its highest state.

Federal contractors. Executive Order 14026, which set a higher contractor minimum wage, was revoked in March 2025, and the Department of Labor no longer enforces it. Its predecessor, Executive Order 13658, still applies to certain older contracts. A Federal Register notice published October 8, 2026 raises that rate to $14.05 per hour, with a tipped cash wage of $9.85, effective January 6, 2027, not January 1.

Form I-9. The current edition is dated 01/20/25 and expires May 31, 2027. No January 1 change, but put a spring check on the calendar.

## AI in hiring, pay transparency, and non-competes

The newest category is AI in hiring, and the headline date comes with an asterisk. Colorado's amended AI law, SB 26-189, takes effect January 1, 2027. The rewrite dropped the original impact assessment and risk management requirements and replaced them with disclosure duties. Employers using automated tools for a consequential decision must give a clear notice before use, send a second one within 30 days of an adverse outcome the tool materially influenced, offer a way to correct bad data and request human review, and keep compliance records for three years. Only the attorney general can enforce it, with civil penalties up to $20,000 per violation.

Two things complicate the date. Enforcement is paused: in April 2026 a federal court in Colorado approved a stipulation between xAI, which sued over the law, and the attorney general, under which the attorney general will not enforce or investigate violations while the challenge is pending, with a 14-day buffer after any ruling on xAI's preliminary injunction motion, which is due within 28 days after final rules are adopted. The U.S. Department of Justice intervened in the case the same month. And the rules are not final: the attorney general published proposed rules on August 11, 2026, with a hearing and comment deadline of October 26. Build the notice and review process now, with the understanding that, as of this writing, the state is not enforcing the law and the final rules were still pending.

Illinois has required notice when AI is used in employment decisions since January 1, 2026. Our January 20 article will cover what to use, what to skip, and what is now regulated.

Pay transparency keeps spreading, but mostly not on January 1. Our August 26, 2026 article covered the rules in force at the time across this footprint. The main addition since then is Virginia, whose law took effect July 1, 2026, applies to every employer regardless of size, requires a good-faith pay range in postings, and bans salary history questions. On non-competes, we did not confirm any new restriction taking effect January 1, 2027, in the eight states above. Outside them, Illinois raises its earnings threshold for enforceable agreements from $75,000 to $80,000 on that date under a schedule written into its 2021 law. If you hire there, check the offer letter template.

## What sophisticated multi-state employers do differently

**They keep the list by state, not by topic.** One page per state with the headcount, the minimum wage, the exempt floor, the leave contribution split, and the posting rules. When a recruiter opens a role in Maryland, that sheet tells them what the ad and the first paycheck have to look like.

**They run a test payroll in December.** The first January run is not the place to learn that Maryland's deduction line was never built or that the Massachusetts employer share moved. A dry pass against the final register of the year catches it with time to fix.

**They put a date on the unknowns.** The IRS limits, New Jersey's rates, Colorado's final rules: each has a known announcement window. The list carries a check-back date next to every pending item instead of a blank.

## What an HR partner handles and what stays in-house

An HR outsourcing partner typically carries the tracking: watching the state pages, updating payroll tables, posting the new notices, revising handbook language, and running the test payroll. What stays in-house is the set of decisions a vendor cannot make for you. Whether to lift pay bands above the new minimum so a two-year employee still earns more than a new hire. Whether to file for a private leave plan. How wide a posted range should be. And the call on keeping an AI screening tool in service in California and Colorado once disclosure duties apply. The outside team brings the mechanics. Leadership owns the choices.

## What this means for boards and CEOs

First, ask for the January 1 list at the December meeting, by state, with a named owner for each line and a check-back date for every item still pending. If it does not exist, that is the finding.

Second, ask for the date the test payroll ran and what it caught. A clean answer takes thirty seconds. A missing one tells you the first live run of 2027 is the rehearsal.

Third, treat the AI-in-hiring rules as a question about which tools the company uses rather than a compliance detail. California's rules are live on January 1 for covered employers. Colorado's put notice and review duties directly on the employer, even though enforcement there is paused until the rules are final and the court has ruled, and our read is that more states will copy the model.

January 1 does not surprise anyone who is looking. The dates are published, the numbers come out on a schedule, and the agencies post the rest. Companies that get caught in February were checking one state's list when they needed five.
