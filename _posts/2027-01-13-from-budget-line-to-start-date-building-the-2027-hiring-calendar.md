The 2027 budget is approved. Somewhere in it is a headcount line. Say it reads 14. That number survived three rounds of review. Finance tested it against revenue. The board asked whether it was too many. Nobody asked the one question that decides whether those 14 people exist by the time the business needs them: on what date does each search have to open?

<!--more-->

A headcount budget is a count. Hiring is a sequence with three clocks, and the budget sets none of them. The first clock runs from the day a requisition opens to the day a candidate accepts. SHRM's 2026 recruiting benchmarking, drawn from more than 4,600 organizations, puts the median at 39 calendar days for nonexecutive roles. The second clock is the notice period the new hire owes a current employer. The third is the ramp, the stretch between the first day and the day the person carries a full load. Only the first shows up in most hiring reports, and it stops at the offer rather than the start.

That gap is where third-quarter hiring problems come from. When a leadership team says in August that it is behind on hiring, the usual cause is neither a weak recruiter nor a soft market. It is that the number approved in January was never turned into dates, so every search opened when someone got around to it. This piece is about doing that conversion once, in January, for every head in the plan. It stays on dates, lead times, and sequencing; our January 2026 article on workforce planning and the July 2026 mid-year reset cover the forecasting side.

## Why a headcount number is not a plan

The number hides three facts. Roles do not fill at the same speed. Time to fill is measured to the offer, and nobody starts work on the day they accept. And a new hire's first month is not a productive month, which in a regulated plant is a rule rather than an opinion.

The market adds pressure of its own. SHRM's State of Recruiting, published in October 2025, said 56 percent of recruiting executives named talent shortages as a challenge, and SHRM's 2025 Talent Trends research found 51 percent of organizations reported too few applicants. In every month of 2026 reported so far, the Bureau of Labor Statistics has counted more open jobs than completed hires, roughly 7 million openings against 5 million hires in a typical month. More jobs sit open than get filled.

The benchmarks also measure different things. SHRM counts from the requisition to the accepted offer. LinkedIn measures from the posting to the day the employer reports the job filled. Retained search firms track days to placement. None of these includes the notice period or the ramp. A planner who takes a 39-day benchmark and books the start date 39 days out has lost at least a month before the search even begins.

## Lead times by role

How long it takes to fill a job depends on the job. The data supports four rough tiers.

Technicians and production roles sit at the fast end. SHRM's 39-day nonexecutive median is the best current anchor. A 2023 study by the Josh Bersin Company and AMS found some jobs filled in 14 days while many stayed open two to three months or more.

Engineers take longer. A LinkedIn analysis of thousands of confirmed U.S. hires, published in August 2023, put the median time to hire for engineering at 60.8 days from posting to filled, the third-longest of ten job functions. The same Bersin and AMS research found energy and defense companies had the longest average time to hire of any industry at 67 days in early 2023, against a 44-day average across all industries. Those figures are a few years old; the 2026 SHRM median is the newer anchor, and the order of the tiers is the point.

Quality leaders and other managers fall in the middle. LinkedIn's 2023 analysis put management at a 57.7-day median. SHRM's 2025 benchmarking reported the median for executive roles at 45 days, down from 60 in 2022, and its 2026 report says the executive figure was unchanged from the prior year. A quality director in a GMP plant usually runs like a management search on the front end and like an executive hire on the back end, because the notice period is longer.

Site heads and other senior leaders are the slow tier. Clockwork, a software vendor serving retained search firms, analyzed tens of thousands of retained searches run between 2020 and 2023 and found an average of 117 days from search start to placement, just under four months. Individual contributor searches averaged 95 days and board searches 155. The cost tracks the time: SHRM's 2025 benchmarking put the median cost per hire at 10,625 dollars for executive roles and 1,200 dollars for nonexecutive roles.

## Working backward from the date that matters

Start with the date the business needs the person productive, not the date the budget assumes they start. Then subtract three blocks of time.

The ramp comes first because most companies skip it. Gallup wrote in 2021 that, in its experience, new employees typically take around 12 months to reach their peak performance potential. Newer data points the same way. An APQC study published in October 2025 found 52 percent of employees who had an ideal onboarding experience reached full productivity in less than a month, compared with 41 percent of everyone else. So even with the best start, about half took longer than that.

Our November 4, 2026 piece on the first 90 days laid out the structure. Plan 30 days for a technician on a documented training path, 90 days for an engineer or a manager, and treat 90 days as the floor for a leader.

Regulated plants have no choice on this. Under 21 CFR 211.25, every person engaged in drug manufacturing must have the education, training, and experience, or a combination of them, to perform their assigned functions, training must cover the particular operations the employee performs, and the plant must have an adequate number of qualified personnel. A new hire in a GMP facility is not a qualified person on day one. The ramp is the qualification period, and the regulation ties headcount to it.

The notice period comes second. Two weeks is a convention in the United States, not a law. SHRM's employment law guidance from 2018 describes two weeks as the standard, notes that most states follow at-will employment, and adds that employers commonly ask executives and employees with unique skills for four weeks. Plan 14 days for individual contributors and 28 for leaders. In our experience, senior candidates also negotiate a start date that lets them finish a project or a quarter, which pushes the real figure past a month.

Time to fill comes last. The role-level figures above are a starting point. Your own last three fills in the same role are a better one.

Here is the arithmetic. A role with a 60-day time to fill, a two-week notice period, and a 30-day ramp needs its search open about 104 days before the person has to be productive. If that date is April 1, 2027, the search opens on December 18, 2026, which is already behind you. Three worked examples:

A maintenance technician who must be productive by May 3, 2027. Use the SHRM median of 39 days to fill, 14 days of notice, and a 30-day ramp. That is 83 days. The search opens February 9.

A process engineer who must be productive by July 1, 2027. Use LinkedIn's 61-day engineering median, 14 days of notice, and a 90-day ramp. That is 165 days. The search opens January 17.

A site head who must be productive by October 1, 2027. Use the 117-day retained search average, 28 days of notice, and a 90-day ramp. That is 235 days. The search opens February 8.

Each input is a median or an average, so roughly half of real searches run longer. A company that wants better odds than a coin flip adds a buffer of two to four weeks to each row, at the front, where it costs nothing.

## Where contract and contract-to-hire fill the gap

The calendar will surface rows where the search-open date has already passed, and rows where the needed date is fixed by something the company does not control: a customer start, a validation campaign, a contract award. That is the contract case. The American Staffing Association reports that nearly 2.2 million temporary and contract employees worked for U.S. staffing companies in an average week in 2024, that staffing firms hired 12.7 million people during 2023, and that 40 percent of staffing employees work in higher-skilled occupations. The association says clients use staffing for flexibility and access to talent.

Contract-to-hire does one more thing, and our September 30, 2026 piece on it as a risk management tool covered the mechanics. It turns the gap into a working interview. A plant that needs a validation engineer by June and cannot open the search until March can bring in a contractor in April, keep the search running, and convert the contractor if the work proves out. The calendar should name that fallback in January.

Manufacturing has the most to gain. The Manufacturing Institute and Deloitte projected in April 2024 that 1.9 million manufacturing jobs could go unfilled by 2033. A calendar does not change the labor pool. It changes whether the company is early or late to it.

## The one-page calendar

One page, one row per budgeted head, nine columns: the role, the hiring manager, the date the business needs the person productive, the ramp in days, the notice period in days, the expected time to fill in days, the resulting search-open date, the current status, and the fallback if the open date slips. Sort the rows by search-open date rather than by department. The top of the page is this month's work.

Then the monthly check. Once a month, in the operating review rather than an HR staff meeting, the page gets three tests: which search-open dates passed without a requisition, which active searches have run past their expected time to fill, and which fallbacks need to trigger now. The answers take ten minutes, and they replace the August surprise with a February decision.

## What sophisticated employers do differently

**They date every head, not every quarter.** "Q2" is not a date. A hiring manager who is told the role is a second-quarter hire will open the search in May. The calendar forces a day, and the day forces the search-open date.

**They use their own lead times before anyone else's.** The benchmarks in this article are a floor for companies with no history. A company with three years of requisition data knows its own time to fill by role, and for the roles that matter most it is usually longer than the published median.

**They open the slowest searches first.** A site head search that opens in February lands a leader in the fall. One that waits until the technicians are hired gets its leader next year. Sequencing by lead time instead of by org chart is the biggest change most companies can make.

**They decide the fallback in January.** Whether to bring in a contractor gets answered calmly in a planning session or badly in a crisis. The fallback column settles it once, per role, in advance.

**They put the calendar in the operating review.** A hiring plan that lives in HR gets reviewed when HR has time. One that sits next to the production schedule and the cash forecast gets reviewed every month, because the people in the room own the dates.

## What this means for boards and CEOs

First, ask for the one-page calendar by January 31. Every budgeted head, a needed-by date, a search-open date, and an owner. If the document does not exist, that is the finding, and it is fixable in two weeks.

Second, ask which search-open dates have already passed. Each one is a role that will be late unless a contract fallback or a shorter ramp is decided now.

Third, put the calendar on the monthly operating agenda for the year. Three tests, ten minutes, every month. The point is to make sure the approved number turns into people on the dates the plan assumed.

The budget says 14. It does not say when. A company that turns the count into dates in January knows by February which roles are on track, which need a contractor, and which need a search that should have opened last month. A company that does not finds out in August, when the number stops being a plan and starts being an excuse.
