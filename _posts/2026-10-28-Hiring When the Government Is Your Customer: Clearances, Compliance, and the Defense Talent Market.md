A mid-sized defense supplier wins the contract it has chased for two years. The celebration lasts about a week. Then the math arrives: the program needs forty cleared engineers, the fastest 90 percent of Top Secret investigations took 227 days last quarter, and every competitor on the same program is recruiting from the same short list of people. The company did not lose the talent war on award day. It lost the war eighteen months earlier, when it decided to wait for the contract before building the bench.

<!--more-->

Selling to the government is unlike selling to anyone else, and hiring for government work is unlike hiring for anything else. The customer decides who is allowed to do the work, how long it takes to authorize them, and what your company must prove about itself before it can even compete. Those rules shrink the candidate pool, stretch the hiring timeline, and raise the cost of every seat.

The National Defense Industrial Association has been tracking the strain for years. In its Vital Signs 2025 report, 54 percent of government respondents and 41 percent of industry respondents named finding and keeping talent as the top pressing issue facing the defense industrial base. The 2026 edition, surveying 469 government and 1,177 private sector respondents, found talent still ranked first among government respondents at 51 percent. Whatever else changes in the defense economy, the people problem stays at the top of the list.

If you run a pharmaceutical plant or a medical device line, do not skip this one. Clearances and GMP compliance are cousins. Both narrow who can do the work, both add months to the calendar, and both reward the employers who plan around the constraint instead of fighting it. We will come back to that.

## The cleared talent market, by the numbers

Start with what the scarcity costs. The 2026 ClearanceJobs Security Clearance Compensation Report put average total compensation for cleared professionals at $126,125, a record high, up nearly 6 percent in 2025. Hold a full scope polygraph, the most restrictive access level, and the average jumps to $149,875, roughly $30,000 above other cleared workers. The market prices access the way it prices any scarce credential: the harder it is to obtain, the more it pays.

Now the timeline. Defense Counterintelligence and Security Agency data reported in early 2026 showed the fastest 90 percent of industry Secret clearance cases taking 156 days, and Top Secret cases taking 227 days. Those are the quick ones. Complex cases and adjudication backlogs run longer. An employer who starts recruiting when the contract is signed is signing up for a half year or more of paying salaries, or leaving seats empty, while the government decides who can sit in them.

Retention is the third pressure. In the same ClearanceJobs survey, 78 percent of cleared professionals said they were likely to change jobs within a year. That number should stop every defense executive cold. The credential your program depends on walks out the door with the person, and the person knows exactly what the credential is worth. Roughly two thirds of respondents named higher pay as the top driver of satisfaction. Cleared talent is not just scarce. It is scarce, mobile, and well informed.

## The rules that make defense hiring different

Three mechanics separate this market from commercial hiring, and each one deserves a place in workforce planning.

Clearance sponsorship is a chicken-and-egg problem. An individual cannot simply apply for a clearance. An employer with a qualifying contract must sponsor the investigation. So companies want candidates who are already cleared, cleared candidates command a premium, and the pool of people willing to wait out a 227-day investigation for an entry ticket stays thin. Reciprocity, the principle that a clearance granted by one agency should transfer to another, is supposed to ease this. In practice it stumbles. A GAO review found 28 of 31 surveyed agencies reporting that their systems at times lacked the information needed to honor a clearance granted elsewhere, and the government could not reliably measure how often transfers were honored at all. Even a cleared hire can sit idle while paperwork crosses agencies.

Export controls shrink the pool before clearances even enter the picture. Under ITAR, the arms trade regulations, work involving controlled defense technology is generally limited to U.S. persons: citizens, green card holders, and certain protected individuals. Many roles never require a clearance but still carry this restriction. For engineering teams, that rule quietly removes a large share of the national technical workforce, including many of the foreign-born engineers commercial employers hire freely.

Compliance now flows down to the smallest shop in the chain. The Pentagon's Cybersecurity Maturity Model Certification program, CMMC, reaches a defense industrial base of some 200,000 companies, with about 8,350 medium and large firms slated for third-party assessments at the middle tier. The department suspended the second phase of the rollout in July 2026 and opened a program review, citing cost and burden on smaller companies. But self-assessments and the underlying federal cybersecurity standard remain in force. For a 60-person machine shop, that means hiring or renting security expertise it never needed before, because the prime above it must verify the whole chain. Compliance capability has become a talent requirement in its own right.

## Two collisions: skilled trades and emerging tech

Defense demand is rising at the exact moment the broader trades pipeline is thinnest. Deloitte and The Manufacturing Institute project U.S. manufacturing will need as many as 3.8 million new workers by 2033, and warn that 1.9 million of those roles could go unfilled. Defense hires from inside that shortage, not apart from it.

Shipbuilding shows the collision at full scale. EY's analysis of the submarine industrial base found it expects demand for 100,000 additional trade workers over the next decade, while yards deliver roughly 1.2 to 1.4 attack submarines a year against a stated need of three. Washington has noticed: GAO reports the Navy put more than $5.8 billion into the shipbuilding industrial base from fiscal 2014 through 2023, with another $12.6 billion planned through 2028. Money can fund training pipelines. It cannot conjure a welder in a week, and it cannot stop a trained one from taking a commercial job closer to home.

The aerospace and defense sector as a whole tells the same story with different numbers. The AIA and McKinsey talent study put the U.S. workforce at 2.23 million in 2024, growing 2.9 percent, with attrition holding near 15 percent and the sharpest shortages in engineering and skilled trades. Growth and leakage at the same time: the sector is filling the tub with the drain open.

Layer on the emerging tech fight. The engineer who understands autonomy, AI, or advanced sensing is already among the most recruited people in the economy. Ask that person to also hold a clearance, also satisfy ITAR, and also accept a facility job in a specific zip code, and the pool shrinks to a puddle. Commercial employers offer remote work, faster starts, and often bigger equity. Defense employers cannot match those terms, which means they must compete on something else and plan much further ahead.

## If you hire under GMP, you already know this story

Here is the section for readers who will never sponsor a clearance. Regulated hiring is one discipline with different uniforms.

A life sciences company staffing a sterile manufacturing line faces the same structural problem as a defense prime staffing a program office. The rules define who counts as qualified. Training and documentation stretch onboarding from weeks to months. Losing one qualified person costs far more than a commercial employer would guess, because the replacement must clear the same slow gate. Cell and gene therapy makes the pattern vivid: more than 2,000 therapies in clinical trials, up from under 300 in 2018, chasing a workforce where the worst gaps sit in manufacturing and quality control, and only about 7 percent of community colleges even offer biotech degrees. Swap "cleared" for "GMP-qualified" and the sentences still work.

The transferable lesson is the same in both worlds: design the hiring process around the constraint. Employers who treat the clearance or the qualification as an annoying final step lose months on every hire. Employers who treat it as the organizing fact of their talent strategy build pipelines early, protect the people who already carry the credential, and price the premium into their plans from the start. The constraint does not move. Your process can.

## What sophisticated organizations do differently

**They build the bench before the contract lands.** Winning bidders staff programs with people identified months earlier: cleared candidates kept warm, near-cleared candidates sponsored early on existing contracts, and trainees moving through trade schools on the company's dime. Waiting for award day means paying the 156-to-227-day tax in the program's most visible early months.

**They treat retention as the cheapest clearance strategy.** With 78 percent of cleared professionals open to moving, the math is blunt: a raise and a real career path cost less than a half-year vacancy plus a new investigation plus a recruiter fee. Every cleared or qualified employee who stays is a hire you do not have to make in the market described above.

**They price the premium in, not out.** Cleared compensation averages $126,125 and climbs every year. Bidding a program at commercial pay rates and hoping is how companies end up staffing at month nine. Sophisticated firms build the documented premium into the bid, the same way they price titanium.

**They use contract staffing where the rules allow it.** Cleared contractors and GMP-experienced contract professionals can bridge surges, cover investigations in progress, and de-risk uncertain program timelines. The care point: clearances belong to positions and sponsors, not to staffing paperwork, so the arrangement must be structured with the facility clearance and contract terms in view. Done right, contract talent turns a hard constraint into a flexible one.

**They sell what commercial employers cannot.** Mission, stability, and hard problems still move people, especially mid-career engineers tired of ad-tech. Defense and life sciences employers who tell that story well, and pair it with credible training pathways for trades roles, recruit from pools their competitors never reach.

## What this means for boards and CEOs

First, make the constraint visible in planning. Every board of a company selling to the government, or operating under GMP, should see a simple map: which roles require the credential, how long the gate takes, who holds it today, and what the pipeline looks like 12 to 18 months out. If that map does not exist, the next contract win is also a staffing crisis.

Second, fund retention like the capital investment it is. The premium for cleared and qualified talent is not a cost overrun. It is the price of admission to the market you chose. Companies that budget for it keep their people. Companies that do not become the training ground for companies that do.

Third, treat compliance readiness as a talent question. CMMC, ITAR, and quality systems all resolve to the same thing: people who know how to do regulated work, hired before the auditor or the contracting officer asks. Small suppliers especially should decide now whether to build, borrow, or buy that capability.

The defense talent market will stay tight for the rest of the decade. The demand signal is public, the timelines are published, and the constraint is stable. That is actually good news. A constraint you can see coming is a constraint you can plan for, and in regulated markets, the planners win.
