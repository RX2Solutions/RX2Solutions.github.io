Four in ten U.S. employees hear recognition from their manager a few times a year or less. That figure comes from a Gallup and Workhuman survey of more than 7,600 workers in 2022, and the Achievers Workforce Institute's 2025 survey found more than half of employees recognized a few times a year or less. For many of those people, the year-end thank-you is one of the few times anyone in charge says anything at all. Which makes it strange that so many companies hand that moment to a gift card and a mass email.

<!--more-->

The ritual is familiar. A bonus if the year allowed it. A holiday party. A gift card loaded with a round number. An all-hands note from the CEO thanking "the entire team" for "everything you do." In a late-2023 survey of 202 U.S. companies, the most recent of its kind, Challenger, Gray & Christmas found 66 percent offered a bonus, and 76 percent of those were paying the same amount as the year before. None of that is wrong. It is just not recognition, and recognition is what the retention research keeps pointing at.

This piece follows our November 18 article on the January resignation, which made the case that senior people decide to leave in the fall and wait for the bonus to clear. The same window applies down the org chart. Robert Half's June 2026 survey of more than 2,000 employed U.S. workers found 46 percent planned to look for a new job in the second half of the year. Those decisions get made in the fourth quarter, and recognition is one of the cheapest levers a company can pull inside that window. Most pull it badly.

## What recognition does to turnover

Gallup and Workhuman tracked the career paths of nearly 3,500 employees from 2022 to 2024 to test whether recognition changes who stays. It does. Well-recognized employees were 45 percent less likely to have turned over after two years. Those whose recognition met at least four of Gallup's five quality standards were 65 percent less likely to be looking or watching for another job, and nine times as likely to be engaged as people whose recognition met none.

The 2022 study put numbers on the other side of the ledger. Employees who hear from their manager a few times a year or less were 74 percent more likely to say they did not plan to be at the organization in a year, and five times as likely to be actively disengaged.

Then there is the cost. Gallup's 2024 analysis priced replacing leaders and managers at around 200 percent of salary, technical roles at 80 percent, and frontline workers at 40 percent. Gallup's older 2019 estimate gave a range of one-half to two times annual salary. Against that, a manager saying a specific thing to a specific person costs a few minutes.

The trend is going the wrong way. The Achievers Workforce Institute, a recognition vendor's research arm, surveyed 3,600 workers in five countries for its 2025 report and found only 23 percent felt meaningfully recognized. The share recognized weekly fell to 19 percent from 29 percent the year before, and the 2026 edition puts it at 17 percent. Recognition from managers, which the report calls the most impactful source, dropped from 20 percent to 15 percent in 2025 and recovered only to 19 percent in 2026. Fewer than one employee in five hears from a manager regularly.

## A program is not a practice

Larger companies usually have a recognition program of some kind. O.C. Tanner's 2025 State of Employee Recognition report, drawn from 2,815 employees at larger organizations in five countries, found 75 percent of organizations recognize service anniversaries and 63 percent recognize top performers. The same report found 54 percent of employees say recognition can still feel like an empty gesture, even where a formal program exists.

That gap is the whole story. A program is a budget line and a calendar: five-year pins, an employee of the quarter, a points platform, a holiday gift. A practice is a manager who notices something and says so, close to when it happened, in enough detail that the person knows it was seen. Programs are bought. Practices are built. In the 2022 Gallup and Workhuman survey, only 36 percent of employees said their organization had a recognition program at all, just 19 percent strongly agreed recognition was an important part of their culture, and 81 percent of leaders said it was not a major strategic priority.

Leadership attention has improved since then. By 2024, 42 percent of senior leaders strongly agreed with the value of recognition, up from 28 percent in 2022. Employee experience did not follow. Just 22 percent of employees say they get the right amount, unchanged from 2022, and 55 percent either receive none or receive recognition that meets none of Gallup's five standards. Leaders decided it mattered. They did not decide who would deliver it, and managers think they already are. In the 2022 survey, 61 percent of managers said they give recognition at least a few times a week, while 40 percent of employees said they get it a few times a year or less.

Gallup names the five standards: recognition should be fulfilling, authentic, equitable, embedded in the culture, and personalized. Personalized is the one the year-end ritual fails first. Only 10 percent of employees have ever been asked how they like to be recognized. O.C. Tanner's 2025 data found 74 percent say a message written for them in particular makes recognition more valuable. A gift card is the same for everyone by design. So is the all-hands email.

Who it comes from matters too. A Gallup survey published in 2016, old enough to treat as context, asked employees to name the source of their most memorable recognition: 28 percent said their manager and 24 percent said a high-level leader or CEO. The Achievers data calls manager recognition the most impactful source. The year-end thank-you usually skips the direct manager and waters down the person at the top.

## Why year-end is the worst time to be generic

Gallup's survey of more than 43,000 U.S. workers in the first half of 2026 found 31 percent engaged, unchanged from 2025 and down from 36 percent in 2020, a drop Gallup puts at about 8 million fewer engaged employees. As of August 2026, Gallup's retention indicator showed 53 percent of U.S. employees watching for or actively seeking a new job. The fourth quarter is when the undecided decide.

Now add the finding from our November 18 piece. Gallup's 2019 research on voluntary turnover found 51 percent of people who quit said no manager or leader had talked with them about their satisfaction or their future in the three months before they left. December sits inside that window for every January resignation. For many companies, the year-end message is the one scheduled moment when leadership addresses everyone, and a sentence written for everyone applies to no one in particular.

The money does not fix this. SHRM's 2023 review found annual bonuses tend to delay departures rather than prevent them. The 2022 Gallup and Workhuman report found about one in five employees receive a monetary reward with their recognition, and noted that tying a reward to a particular achievement sends a different message than giving everyone a bigger paycheck at the end of the quarter. The same report found 29 percent of employees prefer recognition in private, 7 percent prefer it in public, and 64 percent want a mix. A company-wide email is identical for all three groups.

O.C. Tanner's 2026 report, drawn from more than 4,200 employees in 10 countries, found that when employees feel leaders do not know their recognition preferences, their odds of thriving at work drop by 89 percent. When appreciation feels intentional, they are eight times as likely to trust the organization. Generic recognition is not neutral. For the 40 percent who rarely hear from their manager, a template thank-you confirms that the company's version of noticing is a template.

## What it looks like on a plant floor, in a lab, on a defense program, and on a service team

**On the plant floor.** Gallup's 2022 industry breakdown found only one in 10 manufacturing employees said recognition was an important part of their organization's culture, trailing education and healthcare at 17 percent each. The upside is just as large. A 2023 Gallup and Workhuman analysis estimated that doubling the share of employees recognized in a given week could cut safety incidents by 22 percent and absenteeism by 22 percent, and for a 10,000-worker manufacturer put the value of fewer missed days at about 3.8 million dollars. The practice is a shift supervisor naming three people in the huddle and saying what each one did: the changeover that ran clean, the near miss that got reported instead of buried, the line that hit rate without overtime. By name, in December, from the person they report to.

**In the lab.** In our experience, credit in a lab flows to the project or the principal investigator. The analyst who caught drift in a stability trend before it became an out-of-specification result usually hears about it from the QA reviewer and no one else. A year-end thank-you in a regulated lab should name the method, the batch, or the audit that went clean because of one person's work. "Thanks for a great year of science" tells a bench scientist nothing. "The investigation you closed in March is why the September inspection had no findings" tells them everything.

**On a defense program.** Work on a cleared program cannot be described in a company-wide email, and the people doing it know that. Praise in the all-hands is general by necessity. That leaves the private note from the person in charge as the only recognition that can carry detail, and it fits the 29 percent of employees who prefer recognition in private anyway. Say it one on one, in the room where it can be said.

**On a service team.** O.C. Tanner's 2026 report found frontline industries have the lowest rate of recognition champions, despite often needing recognition most. A service lead who names the account, the day, and what the customer said afterward is doing more for retention than the gift card that arrives the same week.

## What sophisticated employers do differently

**They separate the gift from the thank-you.** The gift card, the bonus, and the party are fine. The mistake is letting them stand in for recognition. Run both, and never let the holiday budget become the reason no one wrote a note.

**They push the thank-you down to the manager and give it a deadline.** The CEO's email reaches everyone and lands on no one. A manager's words reach one person and land hard. The companies that get this right ask every people manager to deliver one individual piece of recognition to each direct report before year-end, and HR tracks whether it happened.

**They tie it to a named event and a named outcome.** "Great job this year" is a greeting card. "The way you handled the April deviation is the reason we passed the audit" is recognition. The difference is a date and a result.

**They ask people how they want it.** Nine in ten employees have never been asked. One question in a Monday huddle, private or public, written or spoken, fixes that for the cost of a conversation.

**They keep going in January.** A thank-you that shows up once a year is a program. One that shows up most weeks is a practice. Companies that retain people treat the year-end note as the first entry in a habit rather than the last.

## What this means for boards and CEOs

Three things belong on the agenda before December 31.

First, ask every people manager to deliver individual recognition to each direct report before the holiday break, in person or in writing, naming one thing that person did this year and why it mattered. Have HR report completion by manager. The content stays private. The fact that it happened is a management metric.

Second, take the key-person list from the November 18 piece and write to each name on it yourself. A short note, in your own words, naming the thing they did. Gallup's data says a high-level leader is the second most memorable source of recognition after the direct manager. For the people whose departure would hurt most, be both.

Third, put a recognition measure on the 2027 dashboard. Gallup's question is the simplest: the share of employees who strongly agree they received recognition or praise for good work in the last seven days. Track it quarterly, by manager, next to turnover.

Thanksgiving week is when most companies remember to say thank you. The research says the words only work when they are specific, timely, and come from someone the employee reports to. A gift card says the company spent money. A manager's note says the company was paying attention. In a quarter when half the workforce is deciding whether to stay, only one of those changes the answer.
