The federal government counts hires and quits every month. In every month of 2026 reported so far, the Bureau of Labor Statistics has tallied roughly 5 million hires and 3 million quits, and it publishes both figures about five weeks after the month ends. Most companies measure their own hiring once a year, in a turnover number that shows up in a January board deck. By the time that deck is printed, the people it describes have been gone for months, and nothing on the page says what to do differently.

<!--more-->

Annual turnover is a lagging number. It tells a leadership team what already happened, and the gap between that and what is happening now is where money leaks. Gallup's retention indicator found that as of August 2026, 53 percent of U.S. employees were watching for or actively seeking a new job. A figure like that does not wait for the fiscal year to close. Neither should the numbers a company uses to see it coming.

Most hiring functions know this and still report annually, partly because nobody agreed on what to count. LinkedIn's Future of Recruiting 2025 report found 89 percent of recruiting professionals agree that measuring quality of hire will become more important, while only 25 percent are highly confident their organization can do it. The problem is definitions and plumbing.

This article closes our Close Strong, Start Ready series with five numbers to judge the 2027 hiring calendar by, each with a plain definition, the formula in words, a verified benchmark where one exists, the trap that makes companies report it wrong, and what it means when it moves.

## Why annual numbers arrive too late

Work Institute's 2025 Retention Report, as summarized by ASIS International's Security Management magazine, put as much as 40 percent of all turnover inside the first year of employment. Its 2026 report, drawn from more than 120,000 exit interviews, found nearly three quarters of the reasons people gave for leaving (74.69 percent) fell into categories the firm classifies as preventable. A company that learns in January that first-year attrition ran high is learning about people who left in April. A quarter is the right interval instead: long enough to see a pattern, short enough to change the next one.

## Number one: time-to-fill by role family

**Definition.** The calendar days from the day a requisition is approved to the day the candidate accepts the offer, weekends and holidays included. SHRM's definition is the same except that it starts the clock when the requisition is opened.

**Formula.** For every role filled in the quarter, subtract the requisition approval date from the offer acceptance date. Group the results by role family (production and skilled trades, scientific and quality, professional and administrative, leadership) and report the median for each, with the slowest fill beside it.

**Benchmark.** SHRM's 2022 Talent Access benchmarking report, built on data collected from April to November 2021, put the median time-to-fill for nonexecutive roles at 44 days, with a quarter of organizations under 28 and a quarter over 73. Executive roles ran a median of 60. The nonexecutive average was 54 days, ten above the median. The newer reading is faster. SHRM's 2026 Recruiting Executives Benchmarking brief, released in June 2026 from more than 4,600 organizations, put the nonexecutive median at 39 calendar days.

**The trap.** Measuring from the posting date instead of the approval date. The days between a signed requisition and a live listing are often the slowest in the process, and starting the clock there hides them. The second trap is reporting the average, which lets one 150-day search bury a production line that is filling in three weeks.

**What it is telling you.** When one role family moves and the others hold, look at sourcing or the interview loop for that group. Our July 22, 2026 article covered what a long interview loop costs in lost candidates. SHRM's 2022 stage data showed interviews averaging nine days and offer-to-acceptance four, which leaves most of a 44-day fill in the stages before and between them. When every family moves at once, the problem is upstream: approvals, recruiter capacity, or a hiring freeze nobody announced.

## Number two: offer acceptance rate

**Definition.** The share of job offers that candidates accept.

**Formula.** Offers accepted in the quarter, divided by offers extended in the quarter, pulled from the ATS. Break it out by role family.

**Benchmark.** SHRM's 2022 report put the average acceptance rate at 90 percent for nonexecutive roles and 91 for executive roles. The median in both cases was 100, which likely reflects organizations that extend few offers. Gem, a recruiting software vendor, reports an 82 percent acceptance rate in its 2026 benchmarks, drawn from more than 1,200 companies and 1.2 million hires. The spread between those figures is wide enough that a company's own baseline matters more than either. SHRM's 2025 Talent Trends research adds a complication: 41 percent of organizations cite rising candidate ghosting, where someone stops responding without a formal decline.

**The trap.** Verbal offers that never get entered. Count the offer the day it is made, not the day the letter is signed. A ghosted offer counts as a decline.

**What it is telling you.** Acceptance below the mid-80s in one role family usually means the pay is off, the process was slow enough that the candidate took another offer, or something in the interview put them off. Q1 is the quarter to set that baseline by role family.

## Number three: 90-day retention

**Definition.** The share of new hires still employed 90 days after their start date.

**Formula.** Count every hire whose 90th day fell inside the quarter. Divide the number still employed on that day by the total. The HRIS holds both dates, and the termination record needs a voluntary or involuntary flag.

**Benchmark.** There is no credible published benchmark for 90-day retention, and we are not going to invent one. The closest reliable readings cover the first year. Employ Inc., which runs the Jobvite, Lever, and JazzHR applicant tracking platforms, reported in March 2026 that first-year turnover across 6,640 customers fell from 23.7 percent in 2024 to 12.1 percent in 2025. The decision to leave comes far earlier. A 2023 BambooHR survey of 1,565 full-time U.S. office workers found 70 percent decide within the first month whether a new job is the right fit, and 29 percent know in the first week. Treat the first-year figures as the outer bound and set the 90-day baseline in Q1.

**The trap.** Counting only voluntary exits. A new hire let go at day 60 for performance is a hiring miss. The second trap is measuring the quarter's own starts at quarter end, when most have not reached day 90. The Q1 scorecard reports on people who started in Q4. January starts show up in June.

**What it is telling you.** Our November 4, 2026 article made the case that the first 90 days work as a retention tool. A drop in one department points at the manager or the onboarding plan for that group. A drop across the board points at the hiring profile.

## Number four: cost per hire

**Definition.** The total cost of filling a role, internal and external, divided by the number of roles filled.

**Formula.** Add agency fees, job board and advertising spend, referral bonuses, candidate travel and relocation, the salary and benefits of recruiting staff, and the cost of the applicant tracking system. Divide by hires in the quarter. That is the SHRM method.

**Benchmark.** SHRM's 2022 report put the average cost per hire for nonexecutive roles at 4,683 dollars, with a median of 1,244. Executive hires averaged 28,329 dollars with a median of 8,750. SHRM's 2025 Recruiting Executives Benchmarking, from 2,371 respondents, put the medians at 1,200 dollars for nonexecutive roles and 10,625 for executive roles, and its June 2026 brief reported that executive cost per hire rose substantially in 2026 while nonexecutive costs held steady.

**The trap.** Confusing this number with the price of a bad hire. Cost per hire measures what the company spent to fill a seat. What it loses if the wrong person sits in it is a different figure. Gallup estimated in 2019 that replacing an employee runs one-half to two times annual salary, and Work Institute's more conservative estimate, reported in 2025, is 33 percent of base pay. Our August 5, 2026 article showed how to calculate the full cost of a mis-hire before making one. A low cost per hire paired with weak 90-day retention is not a bargain. It is the cheapest way to lose money.

**What it is telling you.** Rising cost with flat time-to-fill usually means agency reliance is growing. Falling cost with rising time-to-fill means recruiting capacity was cut and the company is paying for it in empty seats.

## Number five: contractor conversion rate

**Definition.** The share of contract and temporary workers who moved to permanent employment during the quarter, out of those who reached a conversion decision.

**Formula.** Contractors converted to permanent in the quarter, divided by contractors whose assignment ended or hit the agreed conversion point. Track the ones who were offered conversion and declined as a separate line.

**Benchmark.** No current, credible benchmark exists for company-level conversion rates, so this is the third number where Q1 sets the baseline. The American Staffing Association's 2014 Staffing Employee Survey found 49 percent took temp work to get a permanent job, 35 percent were offered a permanent position by a client, and two thirds of those accepted. Newer federal data says the intent is still there. The Bureau of Labor Statistics counted 945,000 temporary help agency workers in July 2023, and 27.7 percent said they would prefer a traditional work arrangement.

**The trap.** Counting the conversions without the pool. Five out of six eligible contractors is a pipeline. Five out of forty is a company postponing decisions. The second is forgetting that a converted contractor is a hire. The conversion fee belongs in cost per hire, and the 90th day as an employee belongs in the retention number.

**What it is telling you.** A high rate with strong 90-day retention afterward means the working interview is doing its job. A low rate with a growing contractor headcount means the contract is a holding pattern, and the company is paying a markup to avoid deciding.

## One page, then one meeting

The scorecard fits on a single page: five rows and four columns, showing this quarter, last quarter, the benchmark or the company's own baseline, and a one-sentence note on why the number moved. Each metric gets its definition in a footnote so it cannot drift. In the April meeting, each row gets three questions: which way is it moving, why, and what changes before June 30. The figure the company sets in April is the one it will be judged against in July.

## What sophisticated leadership teams do differently

**They agree on definitions before they look at numbers.** Time-to-fill starts at approval. Offers count the day they are spoken. Retention includes involuntary exits. Nobody changes a definition mid-year because the number looks bad.

**They report medians and ranges, not averages.** SHRM's own data shows a 10-day gap between the average and median time-to-fill and a gap of more than 3,400 dollars in cost per hire. The average hides the outliers, and the outliers are where the decisions are.

**They break every number by role family.** A company-wide offer acceptance rate of 88 percent means little if scientific roles are at 70 and production is at 98. The role family view is what turns a scorecard into a to-do list.

**They tie each number to a budget line.** A rising cost per hire in one family means the agency spend for that family gets reviewed. A falling 90-day retention figure in one plant means that plant's onboarding plan gets rebuilt before the next start date.

## What this means for boards and CEOs

First, ask for the five numbers at the April leadership meeting, with the definitions attached. If the hiring function cannot produce them, that is the finding, and it is fixable in a quarter.

Second, fund the plumbing before demanding the output. Cost per hire requires finance and HR to share a spreadsheet. The 90-day number depends on a voluntary or involuntary flag in the HRIS. Contractor conversion needs the staffing partner to send assignment end dates. None of this is expensive, but all of it has to be asked for.

Third, set the baselines in April and write them down. Offer acceptance has two benchmarks eight points apart, and 90-day retention and contractor conversion have none, so the company's own first-quarter result is the target for the rest of the year.

Closing strong was about finishing 2026 with the right people in place. Starting ready is about knowing, by March 31, whether the people hired in the first quarter are the ones the company meant to hire, whether they are still there, and what each one cost. Five numbers on one page answer all three. The companies that have them in April get to fix the second quarter instead of explaining the fourth.
