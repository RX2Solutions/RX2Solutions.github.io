Two numbers from this fall tell you most of what you need to know about the first quarter. On September 16 the Federal Reserve raised its target rate to a range of 3.75 to 4 percent, and not one member of the committee voted against it. On October 2 the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported that employers added 29,000 jobs in September. The cost of money went up in a market that is barely adding people. Any 2027 plan that assumed cheaper capital and a looser labor market now has to run in the opposite conditions.

<!--more-->

This is our quarterly economic report for the first quarter of 2027. Each quarter we pull the latest government and industry data, test it against what we see in searches and staffing work across life sciences, manufacturing, service industries, and defense, and lay out what it means for the next ninety days. The figures below are current as of early October.

The story has moved since October. Our fourth quarter report described a rotation: factory hiring had turned positive after nearly three years of contraction, service company headcount had turned negative, second quarter growth had come in at 1.5 percent, and three Fed officials had voted to raise rates in July. Since then the rotation has settled and the rate call has hardened. Manufacturing hiring held its gains for a third month. Service employment crawled back above the line. Growth was revised up to 2.2 percent. And the July dissenters won the argument: the full committee raised rates in September and now projects no cut through the end of 2027.

That last point changes the quarter. Q1 is not a quarter to wait for relief. It is a quarter to execute the plan the board approved, in the environment that exists rather than the one the plan assumed.

## The macro picture: firmer growth, stubborn prices, a Fed that moved

Start with growth, which came in stronger than first reported. The Bureau of Economic Analysis revised second quarter real GDP up to 2.2 percent annualized in its third estimate, from 1.5 percent in the second, citing higher investment, consumer spending, and government outlays. First quarter growth was revised to 2.5 percent. The Fed's September projections put full year growth at 2.3 percent for 2026 and 2.4 percent for 2027. Steady, not hot.

Prices are the problem. The PCE price index rose 3.4 percent over the twelve months ending in August, with core at 3.0 percent. August CPI also came in at 3.4 percent, with core at 2.4 percent, while energy ran 16.3 percent above a year earlier and gasoline 27.4 percent higher. The headline is being driven by fuel, and the Fed decided not to wait for fuel to come back down.

That is the interest rate story. On September 16 the Federal Open Market Committee raised its target range by a quarter point to 3.75 to 4 percent on a 12 to 0 vote, calling economic activity solid and inflation elevated. In July three members had dissented in favor of a hike; in September no one dissented against one. The median committee member now expects the funds rate to end both 2026 and 2027 at 4.1 percent, up from June medians of 3.8 and 3.6. The path moved up half a point for next year, and it no longer contains a cut.

Now the labor data. The August Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey showed 7.1 million openings at a 4.3 percent rate, up from 6.9 million a year earlier. Hires were 5.2 million, quits 3.1 million at a 1.9 percent rate, and layoffs 1.6 million. BLS called every one of those readings unchanged or little changed. The freeze is still in place.

Payrolls tell the same story with weaker numbers. Employers added 29,000 jobs in September against an average of 45,000 a month over the prior year, and July and August were revised down by a combined 60,000, with July now showing a loss. Unemployment held at 4.2 percent, participation at 61.8 percent, and average hourly earnings rose 3.0 percent over the year, below inflation. The long-term unemployed, those out of work 27 weeks or more, stayed at 1.9 million and 27.1 percent of everyone without a job.

Separations stayed quiet. Challenger, Gray & Christmas counted 43,281 announced cuts in September, down 18 percent from August and 20 percent from a year earlier, with the year to date total of 573,195 running 39 percent below 2025. Artificial intelligence slipped to fifth among stated reasons for the month but remains the leading reason for the year at 120,136. On the other side of the ledger, announced hiring plans for September were 90,787, the lowest September total since 2011.

The activity surveys show the rotation settling rather than reversing. The ISM Manufacturing PMI came in at 54.5 in September, a ninth straight month of expansion, with the employment index at 52.7, up 1.5 points and in growth for a third month. The prices index jumped 6.8 points to 77.9. The ISM Services PMI registered 54.9, a 27th consecutive month of growth, with business activity cooling to 56.5 from 61.7. Its employment index rose 2.3 points to 50.1, back in expansion for the first time in three months.

Read together: factories are still adding people and paying more for inputs. Service firms stopped shedding people, barely. Both are passing costs along, which in our reading is a large part of why the Fed moved.

## Four industries, four different first quarters

**Life sciences.** The cutting has nearly stopped. BioSpace counted 27 biopharma companies and 2,568 employees affected by layoffs in the third quarter, the lowest quarterly figures since it began tracking in 2024 and a 90 percent drop in affected employees from a year earlier. Demand is moving the other way. Job postings on the site rose 17 percent from January to August, and the eight-month count was up 105 percent from a year earlier in Pennsylvania and 49 percent in New Jersey while California fell 14 percent. Reshoring appears to be behind much of it. Lilly broke ground in September on a 6.5 billion dollar API site in Houston expected to create more than 600 jobs, and a BioPlan Associates study cited by BioSpace found 36 percent of respondents cannot hire process development staff. The free agent pool that defined 2025 is thinning at the same time the plants that need it are being built.

**Manufacturing.** The hiring turn held. The ISM employment index has expanded for three straight months after a contraction that ran nearly three years, and BLS counted manufacturing payrolls up 9,000 in September and 72,000 above the December 2025 low. JOLTS put manufacturing openings at 522,000 in August, up from 416,000 a year earlier. Two cautions. A prices index of 77.9 means input costs are climbing fast enough to undo hiring plans, and the structural gap we described in our August 19 piece has not moved. The difference this quarter is that a higher cost of capital now sits on top of both.

**Service industries.** Headcount stopped falling but did not start climbing. An employment index of 50.1 is a coin flip, and business activity cooled sharply from August. Openings remain deep in the knowledge end: 1.19 million in professional and business services and 1.36 million in health care and social assistance. Health care added 17,000 jobs in September, slower than its 33,000 monthly average, and professional and business services were flat. Challenger's September hiring plans, the weakest for that month since 2011, suggest employers are not staffing up ahead of the holidays. In our search work, demand for operations leaders, finance people, and client-facing professionals did not soften. The layer underneath them is still thin.

**Defense.** The House passed its FY2027 National Defense Authorization Act on July 22 by a vote of 216 to 212, authorizing about 1.15 trillion dollars. The Senate bill, at about 1.14 trillion, has not reached the floor since a procedural vote failed 50 to 46 on July 14, and the Congressional Research Service's September 1 update listed no further Senate action. The government is running on a continuing resolution at fiscal 2026 levels through December 11, and CSIS does not expect full-year spending bills before December. The workforce constraint is unchanged: NDIA's Vital Signs 2026 found 51 percent of government respondents named recruiting and retaining talent among the most pressing issues, and 41 percent of industry respondents cited trouble expanding the technical workforce as a barrier to surging production. Contractors are planning against a topline both chambers broadly agree on and a calendar neither has delivered.

## The first ninety days: executing the 2027 plan at 4 percent

The board approved the 2027 plan in a world that has since changed in three ways. Capital costs more. Raises buy less. And the hiring market is neither loose nor tight. It is frozen.

Take pay first. WTW's survey of 1,650 U.S. organizations projects 3.4 percent salary increase budgets for 2027, below the 3.5 percent delivered in 2026. Mercer's July survey of just over a thousand employers puts the merit budget at 3.2 percent and total increases at 3.5 percent, and the Conference Board found 58 percent of CEOs planning raises of 3 to 3.9 percent. Set those against August inflation of 3.4 percent: the standard raise roughly matches the headline and trails the energy costs employees see every week. WTW found 36 percent hiring at higher salary ranges, 34 percent increasing retention bonuses or spot awards, and 32 percent raising starting ranges.

Now the rate. With the Fed projecting 4.1 percent through the end of 2027, any hire that was justified by a cheaper second half needs a new justification. Capital projects that pencil at 4 percent get built, and the people attached to them get hired. The ones that needed 3.5 percent get deferred, along with their headcount. That sorting should happen on purpose in the first ninety days, not by attrition.

Then the calendar. Our January 13 piece will walk through turning each approved head into a search-open date, with the lead times that make a July start require a January requisition. Our January 27 piece will lay out the five numbers the hiring function should report by March 31. In our experience a market where hires, quits, and openings are all flat rewards the employer that opens the search first.

CEO sentiment supports measured action, not expansion. The Conference Board's confidence measure rose to 52 in the third quarter from 47, with 34 percent of CEOs expecting to add headcount and 61 percent holding capital spending flat.

## What sophisticated operators do differently

**They plan at 4 percent and stop waiting for 3.** The June projections had a cut in 2027. The September projections do not. Operators who built two versions of the plan are running the higher rate version and treating any cut as upside.

**They split the pay budget before January payroll runs.** A 3.4 percent pool spread evenly matches inflation and holds no one. The same money aimed at the roles with the longest time to fill does. A third of employers are already doing this through targeted ranges and retention awards, per WTW.

**They hire into the rotation, not the average.** Manufacturing openings are up 106,000 from a year ago while total openings are up 160,000. Life sciences postings are up 17 percent, with Pennsylvania doubling. Service headcount is flat. Companies with work in more than one of those markets are redeploying before they post.

**They treat the defense calendar as a hiring signal, not a funding signal.** Both chambers have written a topline near 1.15 trillion dollars. Neither has delivered it. Cleared engineering and program leadership searches take months. Starting after the appropriation clears means recruiting against the whole industry at once.

## What this means for boards and CEOs

First, ask whether the 2027 plan still works at the rate the Fed is projecting, not the rate the plan assumed. The board should see the version that runs at 4.1 percent through December 2027 before the quarter ends.

Second, ask for the pay plan, not the pay budget. A 3.4 percent number is a defensible answer in a 3.4 percent inflation year. The question is who gets more than that, who gets less, and whether the people on the first list are the ones a competitor would call. That decision belongs in January, before the compensation cycle locks it in.

Third, ask for the search-open dates. The hiring calendar, the subject of our January 13 piece, turns approved headcount into dates. In a market where BLS describes hires, quits, and openings as unchanged, the employer that opens the search in January is the one with the person in the building by summer.

The economy heading into this quarter is growing at a moderate pace, paying more for energy, borrowing at a higher rate, and reassigning work between industries more slowly than in the fall. None of that argues for a freeze, and none of it supports a broad expansion. It argues for running the plan the board approved, at the rate the Fed set, with the pay budget aimed where it changes an outcome. The companies that do that will not be waiting on a cut the committee has stopped forecasting.
