We published close to 50 articles this year. Most of them argued one thing: the hiring market is not a weather system you wait out. It is a set of mechanics you can see, measure, and work. Twelve months later, the evidence has mostly backed that up, and in a few places it has corrected us. This is the short version of what held, what surprised us, and what we would write differently.

<!--more-->

A quick map of the year. January and February went to the leadership reset. Spring belonged to the CPHI manufacturing series. From July into November we ran a cross-industry series across life sciences, manufacturing, service, and defense. Underneath all of it, the economy kept changing the question. Growth stayed modest, prices stayed stubborn, the Federal Reserve raised rates in September after months of holding, and the industries adding people traded places with the industries cutting them. Our January 6 report will carry the numbers. This piece carries the lessons.

Five of them survived the year. Each one points back to the article that made the case, so the detail is a click away if you want it.

## Lesson one: the skilled talent gap is structural, and the cycle does not fix it

In August we argued that the shortage of skilled manufacturing workers is not a hiring-cycle problem. The Manufacturing Institute and Deloitte projected in 2024 that as many as 1.9 million manufacturing jobs could go unfilled by 2033. The drivers were retirements, a training pipeline that shrank for a generation, and a shift toward digital skills. None of those respond to interest rates.

The rest of the year made the point for us. The Fed held, then hiked, and manufacturing employment grew through the third quarter anyway. Demand for maintenance technicians, controls engineers, and quality staff did not wait for cheaper money. The companies that funded apprenticeships and technical college partnerships in 2024 are staffing 2027 from a pipeline. The ones that waited for the market to loosen are competing for the same thin pool, now at a higher cost of capital.

Our November 11 piece on the veteran talent pipeline is the practical follow-up. Every year a large, predictable group of people leaves the military with documented training and a safety-first work habit, and most manufacturers have no working channel to them.

## Lesson two: process speed decides outcomes, but speed has a floor

In July we wrote that slow hiring loses candidates and rushed hiring loses money, and that the two failures come from the same cause: running one process for every role. Most of the dead time in a search is calendar friction rather than evaluation. Panels that cannot find a shared hour. Debriefs that slip a week. That part is free to fix. The evaluation itself is what a compressed process has to protect.

The benchmark moved in the right direction this year. SHRM's 2026 recruiting benchmarking, drawn from more than 4,600 organizations, puts the median time to fill a nonexecutive role at 39 calendar days. That is a different metric from the time-to-hire figure we cited in July, and we come back to that below. The lesson is unchanged. Thirty-nine days is long enough to lose a strong candidate to a competitor who moved in twenty, and short enough that a company cutting below it should ask what it stopped evaluating.

The December 2 article took this one step further. The companies that keep hiring through December start the year with people already on board, while everyone else opens the same searches in the most crowded week of January.

## Lesson three: the manager is the retention variable, and most companies do not measure it

In August we made the case that voluntary turnover points at the direct manager more reliably than it points at pay or company size. Gallup's long-running research found managers account for at least 70 percent of the variance in employee engagement, a finding first published in 2015 and repeated since. In technical industries the pattern has a specific shape: the best scientist, operator, or engineer gets promoted into management with no training, and the organization loses its top contributor and then the team.

This lesson ran through the whole second half. The First 90 Days piece in November argued that the manager is the product a new hire evaluates in the first quarter. The November 18 piece on the January resignation described how many of the people who quit never had a conversation about their future in their last three months, and why the window to hold that conversation is October through December. The Thanksgiving piece on recognition made the case that the cheapest retention lever available is a manager who notices particular work and says so.

Three articles, one variable. If a leadership team can report attrition by manager, trended over two years, it can act on this. If it cannot, that is the first thing to fix in 2027.

## Lesson four: pay transparency became the market, not a compliance project

In August we counted thirteen states plus Washington, D.C. requiring salary ranges in job postings and argued that compliance was the easy half. Connecticut made it fourteen in October. The harder half was structural: job architecture, compression audits, one national posting standard, and managers who can explain pay.

Candidates settled the question on their own, well before most of the posting laws took effect. Monster's Job Search Deal-Breakers Report, published in the spring of 2026, found 60 percent of job seekers will not apply to a posting that does not include a salary range. A company that leaves the number off is losing six of every ten applicants before the conversation starts, whatever it believes it is protecting.

The December 16 checklist carried this forward into the January 1 changes, where pay disclosure rules sit alongside minimum wage increases, paid leave milestones, and AI hiring laws. The lesson for 2027 is that a posted range is a recruiting asset when it is honest and a liability when it is gamed, and the people reading it most carefully are already on the payroll.

## Lesson five: contract-to-hire grew up as a risk tool, with a sharp edge

In September we described contract-to-hire as a risk management tool rather than a budget workaround: an extended working evaluation for roles with uncertain demand or no internal precedent, and the wrong tool for senior passive candidates and scarce cleared talent, who do not audition.

The market supported the first half. The American Staffing Association's weekly index strung together month after month of year-over-year growth through the summer, after a down year in 2025. Robert Half's survey of more than 2,000 U.S. hiring managers, released in July, found 56 percent expected to hire contract professionals in the second half of the year, most often for specialized project skills and urgent coverage. Companies are choosing flexible capacity on purpose, and the January 13 piece on the hiring calendar shows where it belongs: in the gap between the date the business needs someone and the date a permanent search can deliver them. The sharp edge still applies. A senior passive candidate or a cleared engineer will not take a tryout, and asking them to is how a company loses a finalist it spent four months finding.

## What surprised us

Two things. The first was the Fed. In our October economic report we wrote that the argument inside the committee had shifted from when to cut to whether to hike, and that any 2027 plan built on cheaper capital was resting on a forecast the Fed was no longer making. We did not expect the answer to arrive so quickly. The committee raised rates in September, and two more meetings sit between that decision and the new year. The plans most companies approved this fall will run at whatever rate the Fed sets this winter, and our January 6 report lays out what that means for the first ninety days.

The second was biopharma. After two years of cuts, BioSpace's third-quarter tally found the number of employees affected by layoffs down about 90 percent from a year earlier, the lowest quarterly reading since it began tracking in 2024. The free-agent pool that defined 2025 is thinning at the same time new domestic plants are coming online. Companies that assumed experienced quality and manufacturing talent would stay available through 2027 should revisit that assumption now.

## What we would write differently

Two corrections, offered in the spirit of the standard we hold our sources to. The 44-day figure we used in July was time-to-hire, from an AMS and Josh Bersin Company analysis of roughly 250,000 hires. SHRM's 39-day benchmark measures time-to-fill, which starts the clock earlier, at the requisition. They are different yardsticks, and we should have said so when we compared them. We will use SHRM's going forward and name the metric every time.

The second correction is about advance estimates. The second quarter growth figure we cited in October was the government's first read, and the second quarter came in stronger when the Bureau of Economic Analysis revised it. Advance estimates move. We will say so more plainly when we cite one.

## What carries into 2027

The January pieces pick up where the year leaves off. The Q1 economic report on January 6 reads the fourth quarter data and gives the numbers this piece has deliberately left out. The hiring calendar on January 13 turns an approved headcount number into search-open dates. The AI in hiring piece on January 20 maps what is in use and what is now regulated. The scorecard on January 27 gives the leadership team five numbers to report by March 31.

## What this means for boards and CEOs

First, ask which of the five lessons your company has acted on and which it has only read. The structural gap, the process floor, the manager variable, the posted range, and the flexible layer each come with a specific, measurable action. In our experience, most companies have done two of the five.

Second, carry the year-end conversations into January. The stay conversations, the key-person list, and the recognition that lands are not holiday projects. They are the retention plan for a year in which the raise budget trails inflation and the cost of capital went up.

Third, plan at the rate the Fed set this fall, not the one the budget assumed. Most 2027 plans were drafted on a forecast that changed in September. The version that runs at the current rate is the one worth executing.

Twelve months of evidence did not change our view that hiring is a set of mechanics rather than weather. It sharpened it. The companies that treated it that way in 2026 are starting 2027 with people in the building, a calendar on the wall, and a scorecard due in March. That is what close strong, start ready looks like.
