Every January, the same scene plays out in leadership teams across manufacturing, life sciences, and defense. A plant manager, a quality director, or a program lead walks into the CEO's office in the second or third week of the month and resigns. The CEO is surprised. The resignation letter is dated that morning. In our experience, the decision behind it is often weeks or months old.

<!--more-->

The data on timing is not subtle. Indeed's Hiring Lab found job searches on its platform ran up to 31 percent higher in January 2026 than the early December average, and noted the January spike has recurred every year since 2020 except January 2021. A Robert Half survey released in June 2026 found 46 percent of employed U.S. workers planned to look for a new job in the second half of the year, up from 38 percent six months earlier and 27 percent a year before. At the top of the org chart, Challenger, Gray & Christmas counted 209 CEO exits at U.S. companies in January 2026, a 40 percent jump from December. January 2025, at 222, was the highest January since Challenger began tracking in 2002.

Most of those people made their plans in the fall, then waited. What they were waiting for is the subject of this article, and it is the reason the January resignation is still fixable in November.

## The bonus checkpoint

For many people, the thing worth waiting for is the bonus. A late-2025 poll of 2,000 U.S. workers by JobHire AI, a job-search app, found 48 percent of those expecting an annual bonus were already considering leaving once the money arrived. The poll was published as a LinkedIn post without a methodology, so treat it as a signal rather than a study. The academic framing around it is more useful. Sandy Hershcovis, a management professor at the University of Calgary's Haskayne School of Business, described bonuses as a psychological checkpoint: a clean exit point for someone who has already decided. Bonuses change when people leave more than whether they leave.

A 2023 SHRM Executive Network piece made the same point about retention incentives. Annual bonuses tend to delay departures rather than prevent them. The clearest evidence came from equity, not cash: research cited in that piece found the monthly quit rate rose by half a percentage point immediately after stock options vested. People stay for the money they have already earned, collect it, and go.

For a board or CEO, this reframes the whole year-end period. The bonus you are about to pay is a timer for the people who have already decided to leave. They are counting down to it, and nothing in the payout itself will change their mind, because their mind was made up before the number was even set.

## Why November is the window

Gallup's 2019 research on voluntary turnover found 52 percent of employees who left said their manager or organization could have done something to prevent it. The companion finding is the one that matters for timing: 51 percent of those who left said that in the three months before their departure, neither their manager nor any other leader had a conversation with them about their job satisfaction or their future with the company.

Count back three months from a mid-January resignation. You land in mid-October. The window in which half of all voluntary exits could have been caught by one real conversation is October, November, and December. It is also, in most companies, the busiest stretch of the year: budgets, year-end close, holiday coverage, the board's annual plan review. The conversation that would have kept the quality director does not happen because her boss is building the 2027 plan she was supposed to run.

The choice to leave is rarely about the bonus number. Robert Half's June 2026 survey asked people planning a search what was driving it: better benefits and perks (47 percent), career advancement opportunities (43 percent), remote work options (39 percent), higher salary (35 percent), and burnout (26 percent). Most of those are things a manager can see coming and, in many cases, address before year-end. None of them is addressed by a bigger check in January.

## The signals leaders miss

People who have decided to leave do not announce it, but they do leak it. A 2016 study in the Journal of Management, led by Timothy Gardner at Utah State University, identified 13 observable pre-quitting behaviors that managers can spot. Among them: being less willing to commit to long-term timelines than usual, leaving early more frequently, showing less interest in pleasing their manager, expressing dissatisfaction with the job more often, and losing enthusiasm for the organization's mission. The study tracked employees for 13 months and found that a one-unit increase on the pre-quitting behavior scale raised the odds of voluntary turnover by a factor of 6.48. The same work found the behaviors correlated strongly with manager expectations of turnover, which is a polite way of saying managers often sense it and do nothing.

At the senior level, the tells are more specific and easier to see if someone is looking. A site head who stops pushing for next year's capital projects. A regulatory affairs leader who suddenly wants everything documented and handed off. A program manager in a cleared environment who goes quiet about the recompete. A VP whose calendar shows a rash of midday "personal appointments" in November. None of these is proof. Together, they are the pattern Gardner's research describes, and they show up weeks before the recruiter's phone call turns into an offer.

The leaders who catch these signals do not confront them. They respond with a conversation that should have been on the calendar anyway.

## Why the January counteroffer fails and the November conversation works

When the resignation lands in January, the reflex is the counteroffer. It feels decisive, and in the short run it sometimes works. In the long run, the data is poor. A Robert Half survey of 2,000 U.K. employees found that among those who accepted a counteroffer, 34 percent had left within six months and 74 percent within a year. That fieldwork is a few years old, so pair it with the newest reading. In a Robert Half survey of 500 Australian hiring managers fielded in October 2025, 85 percent said they had extended a counteroffer in the past year, and 32 percent reported that the employee who received one still left within 12 months. Different markets, different years, same shape. A third to three quarters of counteroffers buy you months, not years.

The reason is simple. A counteroffer treats a choice made in October as a pricing problem in January. The person did not leave over money. They left over advancement, scope, workload, or a boss who never asked. The raise fixes none of that, and the employee now knows the company only moved when threatened. The relationship is different from that day forward, and usually worse.

The November conversation works because it reaches the same person before the decision hardens. It is a manager, or for senior roles the CEO, sitting down and asking four questions: what would make the next year better than this one, what is getting in the way of the work you want to do, what would it take for you to be here in three years, and what have we done that you did not like. The answers are rarely surprising. Advancement that stalled. A scope that shrank after a reorganization. A workload that never came back down after the last departure. A promise from 18 months ago that nobody followed up on.

Most of those can be addressed, at least in part, before the bonus clears. The ones that cannot be addressed are just as valuable to hear in November, because they give the company two months to plan a succession instead of two weeks.

## What sophisticated boards and CEOs do differently

**They keep a key-person list and treat it as a living document.** Not the org chart. A short list of the people whose departure would hurt in the next 12 months, with a named interim and a named external backup plan for each. The list gets reviewed in October, before the first resignation.

**They put stay conversations on the calendar.** Every person on the key-person list gets a scheduled conversation between mid-October and Thanksgiving. For direct reports of the CEO, the CEO holds it. The purpose is stated plainly: we want you here, tell us what that requires. Gallup's finding is that half of leavers never got that conversation. The fix is to make it impossible to skip.

**They act on what they hear before year-end.** A stalled promotion, a title that did not match the scope, a development budget that was cut and never restored. These are the things people cite when they leave, and most are cheaper to fix in November than to replace in February. Where the ask is unreasonable, the company says so, and both sides know where they stand.

**They settle the counteroffer policy in advance.** Who gets one, who does not, and what the ceiling is. Working this out in the calm of November beats improvising it in a January meeting where emotion and panic set the number. Some companies conclude that for most roles the answer is no, and put the energy into the November conversation instead.

**They start the quiet succession work in December.** For any key person whose November conversation revealed a risk the company cannot fix, December is when the interim plan gets written, the search partner gets a heads-up, and the board hears about it. In our experience, a leadership search that opens in January rarely lands someone before spring. A search that is quietly prepared in December can.

## What this means for boards and CEOs

First, ask for the key-person list at the next board meeting and, for each name, the date of the last real conversation about their future. If the list does not exist or the dates are more than three months old, that is the finding, and it is fixable in the next 30 days.

Second, put a named interim next to every name on that list before the December meeting. The point is to make sure a single January resignation does not become a six-month leadership gap in a plant, a lab, or a program with a government customer watching.

Third, settle the counteroffer question before anyone resigns. Set the policy, set the exceptions, and name who makes the call. Then redirect the money you would have spent in January into the conversations and fixes that work in November.

The January resignation is a predictable event with a published timeline. The job searches spike in the first week of the year, the CEO exits cluster in the first month, and the bonus payout is the trigger many people wait for. The companies that lose leaders every January are late rather than unlucky. The ones that keep them are holding the right conversation in the right month, before the decision gets made for them.
