Most New Year's resolutions are about adding something: a gym membership, a reading list, a budget. The useful ones are about quitting. Hiring works the same way. Few companies need a new recruiting tool in 2027. They need to stop doing a few things that feel productive, cost real money, and have been measured enough times to know better.

<!--more-->

Here are five, picked because the data on each is clear and because we see every one of them in client conversations most months. Each gets a quick case for why it hurts and a note on what to do instead. None of them requires a new budget line. Most need only a meeting shorter than the one you are sitting in right now.

Fair warning: a few of these will sound familiar from earlier pieces this year. That is the point. The habits that survive twelve months of articles about them are the ones that need a formal retirement party.

## Habit 1: Hiring the resume instead of the job

Too many postings still read like a wish list from 2015: bachelor's degree required, five years of industry experience, three certifications, and a willingness to be the one person who already knows your systems. Then the hiring manager wonders why the pool is thin.

**Why it hurts.** The degree line is the clearest example. A February 2024 study from the Burning Glass Institute and Harvard Business School tracked companies that announced they were dropping degree requirements. The announcements were loud. The hiring barely moved. The researchers found the promised opportunity showed up in fewer than one in 700 hires in 2023.

About 45 percent of the firms made the change in name only, with no real shift in who they brought on, and roughly one-fifth made short-term gains and then slid back. Only 37 percent followed through. Those firms got something for it: workers without a degree had a retention rate 10 percentage points higher than their degree-holding colleagues.

**Do this instead.** Before the posting goes live, write down the three things the person has to be able to do in the first six months. Then ask whether each line in the requirements section predicts those outcomes. If it does not, cut it. Our October 21 piece on skills-based hiring laid out the full method. The short version: hire for what the job needs, test for it, and let the resume be a conversation starter rather than a gate.

## Habit 2: Waiting for the unicorn

Every search has a moment when the hiring manager has seen four good candidates and is still holding out for the one who checks every box. The seat stays empty. The team covers. The manager calls it being selective.

**Why it hurts.** SHRM's 2026 Recruiting Executives Benchmarking, drawn from more than 4,600 organizations, puts the median time to fill a nonexecutive role at 39 calendar days, down from the prior year, with executive roles unchanged from the prior year's 45. Those are the midpoints. The unicorn hunt is the long tail. The wider market is still tight: ManpowerGroup's 2026 survey of 39,063 employers across 41 countries found 72 percent had difficulty filling roles, and 69 percent in the United States.

The cost of the hunt is real too. SHRM's 2025 edition put the median cost per executive hire at 10,625 dollars, up 113 percent since 2017, and found only 20 percent of organizations measure quality of hire at all. So most companies cannot tell whether the extra weeks of hunting produced a better person. They are paying for selectivity they never check.

**Do this instead.** Set a decision date at the kickoff meeting rather than after the fourth candidate. Rank the must-haves against the nice-to-haves before the first interview, and agree in writing on what "good enough to hire" looks like. If the unicorn exists, it will show up inside the window or it will not, and the team has a plan either way. Our July 22 article on interview process length covers the timeline side in detail.

## Habit 3: Letting the counteroffer be the retention plan

Someone valuable resigns. The company matches the outside offer, maybe adds a title, and everyone breathes. The retention plan, it turns out, was waiting for people to quit.

**Why it hurts.** The counteroffer buys months, not years. A 2025 Robert Half survey of 500 Australian hiring managers found that 85 percent had extended a counteroffer in the past year, and 32 percent said the employee who received one still left within 12 months. An older Robert Half poll of 2,000 U.K. employees, fielded in December 2021, was harsher: 34 percent of people who accepted a counteroffer had left within six months, and 74 percent were gone within a year. Different markets, same shape.

The reason is simple, and our November 18 piece on the January resignation walked through it. People rarely leave over pay alone. The real reasons are scope, advancement, workload, or a manager who never asked. A raise does not fix any of those, and now the company knows the employee was looking. In the same Australian data, only 41 percent of employers said they prioritize proactive retention, and 17 percent admitted they lean on the counter when someone resigns.

**Do this instead.** Decide the counteroffer policy in January, in calm conditions, before anyone resigns. Who gets one, who does not, and what the ceiling is. Then move the money. The dollars you would have spent matching an outside offer in the panic of a resignation buy more when they go into a promotion, a scope fix, or a stay conversation held months earlier.

## Habit 4: Treating onboarding as paperwork

Day one: badge, laptop, benefits forms, a tour, a lunch. Day two: good luck. Most companies call that onboarding. The new hire calls it a week.

**Why it hurts.** The new hire is making a decision faster than the company thinks. BambooHR's 2023 survey of 1,565 U.S. office workers found 70 percent of new hires decide whether a job is the right fit within the first month, and 29 percent know within the first week. Employ's 2025 Job Seeker Nation report, the most recent edition to publish the figure, found 36 percent of more than 1,500 U.S. adults had left a job within the first 90 days because of what the report called a mismatch in the hiring process.

The newest evidence cuts both ways. Employ's 2026 Hiring Benchmarks report, drawn from 6,640 customer accounts, found first-year turnover fell from 23.7 percent in 2024 to 12.1 percent in 2025. People are staying put more than they did, which makes the early exits that still happen harder to blame on the market. Gallup's often-cited finding, first published in 2018, was that only 12 percent of employees strongly agree their organization does a great job onboarding. A 2021 follow-up found only 29 percent of new hires felt fully prepared and supported to excel, and that when the manager took an active part, employees were 3.4 times as likely to call their onboarding a success.

**Do this instead.** Treat the first 90 days as part of the hire. Our November 4 article laid out the plan: a named trainer, a visible early win, and a manager conversation at 30, 60, and 90 days that is on the calendar before the start date. The forms still get signed. They just stop being the whole program.

## Habit 5: Benchmarking pay once a year

The compensation review happens in the fourth quarter. Ranges get adjusted, merit budgets get set, and the file closes until next fall. For eleven months, the market moves and the ranges do not.

**Why it hurts.** Payscale's 2026 Compensation Best Practices Report, built on 3,413 responses collected from October to December 2025, put the average pay increase at 3.5 percent, and its August 2026 Salary Budget Survey found employers planning 3.5 percent for 2027, up from 3.4 in 2026. Raises of that size, handed out once a year, cannot keep ranges current when new hires come in at market. The gap shows up as compression: longer-tenured people sitting on ranges set a year or two ago, next to a new colleague who was priced last month.

Now add transparency. Payscale found 57 percent of organizations post salary ranges in job ads, 49 percent are targeting company-wide or public pay transparency in 2026, up from a third the year before, and 40 percent blame unverified salary data from outside sources for driving unfair-pay complaints. If employees can see the numbers and the company only checks them annually, the employees will know first.

**Do this instead.** Keep the annual cycle for merit. Add a quarterly look at the ten roles where you hire most or lose most, and fix compression when it is one adjustment instead of a reorg. Our September 16 piece on salary benchmarking after the market correction has the method.

## How to make the new habits stick

Retiring a habit takes more than agreeing it is bad. A few things make it hold.

**Give each habit an owner and a number.** "Define the job before posting" becomes a one-page scorecard signed by the hiring manager. "Stop waiting for the unicorn" becomes a decision date on every requisition. If no one owns the metric, the old behavior comes back by March.

**Put the review on the calendar before the year gets busy.** A 30-minute look at open requisitions, offers, counteroffers, and 90-day check-ins each month is enough. The companies that keep new habits do not have more discipline. They have a recurring meeting.

**Measure the thing you are trying to fix.** Quality of hire, 90-day retention, counteroffer survival at 12 months, and compression by tenure band. Four numbers, pulled quarterly. Most of them come from systems the company already has.

## What this means for boards and CEOs

First, ask for the four numbers above at the Q1 review. If no one can produce them, that is the finding, and it is cheaper to fix in January than to discover in a resignation letter in June.

Second, settle the counteroffer policy and the time-to-decision rule for leadership roles before the first 2027 search opens. Both are five-minute decisions that get very expensive when they are made under pressure.

Third, treat the first 90 days of every senior hire as a board-visible milestone. The data says most new hires have decided within a month of starting. The company should have a plan that runs at least that long.

None of these habits is a mystery, and none is hard to drop. They survive because they feel like diligence. A longer posting feels careful. A longer search feels selective. A counteroffer feels decisive. A thick onboarding packet feels thorough. An annual pay review feels orderly. The companies that hire well in 2027 will be the ones that stopped mistaking the feeling for the result.
